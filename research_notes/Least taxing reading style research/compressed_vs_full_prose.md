# Compressed/telegraphic vs. full, redundant, well-connected prose: effects on skilled adult readers

Compiled 2026-11-09. Evidence tags used below: **[strong]** = meta-analysis or large replicated body; **[moderate]** = several experiments or one large well-controlled study; **[weak]** = single small study, thesis, preprint, or only a secondary summary seen; **[anecdotal]** = practitioner claim without method. "pop:" names the population (L1 adults unless stated). "outcome:" names what was measured (reading time, comprehension accuracy, recall/learning, subjective effort), because a style can win on one and lose on another.

Method note for the report writer: several publisher pages (PMC, Springer, ScienceDirect, Taylor & Francis) blocked direct fetching. Where I could not open a paper, abstracts were retrieved through the OpenAlex and Europe PMC metadata APIs (verbatim abstracts, flagged "abstract verified" below) or, failing that, through search-engine summaries (flagged "secondary"). Numbers that appear only in a secondary summary are labelled as such and should be re-checked before quoting.

## 1. What happens to reading speed and comprehension when function words are removed? Do direct experiments on telegraphic ("caveman") text exist for skilled adult readers?

### Takeaway
No direct, controlled experiment comparing telegraphic (function-word-stripped) prose with full prose, measuring reading time, comprehension and effort in skilled L1 adult readers, was found; the answer has to be inferred from neighbouring work. The closest direct test is an unpublished thesis on Braille readers (20% of words removed, reading rate up, preference mismatched with performance); the rest is adjacent evidence (headline ambiguity, noisy-channel repair of deletions, text-message abbreviations, simplified/elaborated L2 input, agrammatic aphasia) that points to a trade-off, not a free win.

### Cited Findings
- **[weak; pop: Braille readers; outcome: reading rate, detail recall, preference]** Eskenazi (2011, University of Maryland thesis) removed 20% of words from stories ("telegraphic text") and added context-based scaffolding. Telegraphic text significantly increased reading rate; scaffolding significantly increased memory for story details; user preference "does not coincide with either speed or ability to recall story details". Tactile reading is slower than visual reading, so the bottleneck differs from print; this is a thesis, not peer reviewed. Abstract verified. — [Eskenazi 2011](https://doi.org/10.13016/m2g424)
- **[weak, unverified]** A Michigan State University thesis on "stripped-down" prose (20, 40 and 60% of words deleted) combined with time-compressed audio measured comprehension, learning efficiency, reading rate and reading time. Full text was behind an access wall, so authors, year, population and results are unknown to me. — [MSU thesis record](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/36665/FULL_TEXT/view)
- **[moderate, single lab; pop: skilled "texters"; outcome: eye-movement reading time]** Skilled text-message users read SMS-style sentences (orthographic abbreviations such as "wk" for week, and phonological respellings such as "u" for you) with a reading cost at both the local (word) and global (sentence) level compared with normal sentences; phonological abbreviations cost more than orthographic ones. This is letter-level compression, not function-word deletion, but it shows that non-standard compressed forms cost time even for expert users. Abstract verified; sample size and effect sizes not seen. — [Perea, Acha & Carreiras 2009, QJEP](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902783653)
- **[weak to moderate; pop: adolescents/undergraduates; outcome: literacy correlates]** Use of textisms correlated negatively with reading, nonword reading, spelling and morphological awareness, partly accounted for by texting frequency (production task, not a reading-cost test). A 2023 PRISMA review of 62 textism papers found "great disparity" in results. — [De Jonge & Kemp 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9817.2010.01466.x); [Fernández-Juliá & Gómez Camacho 2023](https://doi.org/10.12795/revistafuentes.2023.23011)
- **[moderate, partly unverified; pop: L1 college readers; outcome: reading time and judgments]** Perfetti, Beverly, Bell & Rodgers (1987) studied "Comprehending newspaper headlines" (J. Memory & Language), the standard test of headlinese (articles and auxiliaries dropped). I could not open the paper. An unlabelled abstract on a profile page (attributed to this study only by inference) reports: brief headline wording leaves sentence structure underspecified, ambiguous headlines took longer than unambiguous ones (supporting a syntactic-ambiguity account over a problem-solving account), fit-to-story judgments were slowed by ambiguity but not implausibility, and readers apparently kept one interpretation and often failed to register the ambiguity. Treat as unverified. — [paper record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596x(87)90110-0); [profile-page abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/scholar/2R5808015/Laura_C._Bell)
- **[moderate; pop: L1 adults; outcome: noticing/repair]** Skilled readers silently repair isolated function-word omissions. Staub, Dodge & Cohen (2019): a repeated "the" was detected on only 46% of trials (66% when both instances were fixated); a repeated noun on 90%; an omitted "the" was also hard to detect. Staub et al. (2025, online 2024): 165 participants read full newspaper articles with nine inserted function-word errors (3 repetitions, 3 omissions, 3 transpositions); the median participant detected 1 of 9. Authors: previous work "dramatically underestimated" failure to notice, and no existing reading model accounts for it. Abstracts verified. These are rare, random omissions in otherwise normal text, not a systematic telegraphic register. — [Staub et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-018-1492-z); [Staub et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02586-1)
- **[moderate, with a methodological critique; pop: L1 adults; outcome: interpretation]** Noisy-channel account (Gibson, Bergen & Piantadosi 2013, PNAS): comprehenders weigh plausibility priors against a model of corruption; they treat deletions and insertions asymmetrically (more willing to posit a missing word than an extra one); non-literal interpretation grows with perceived noise rate. As reported in press coverage of the paper, with a one-edit alternative readers accepted the literal sentence about 50% of the time versus about 97% when two edits were needed (secondary figure). A 2019 CUNY poster argues the effect may reflect answer-plausibility judgments after the sentence rather than parsing, because of the yes/no question task. — [PNAS abstract](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216438110); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2013/decoding-noisy-language-0429); [Kane & Slevc poster](https://www.colorado.edu/event/cuny2019/sites/default/files/attached-files/c63_kane_slevc.pdf)
- **[moderate (reading time vs propositions); secondary summary seen]** Kintsch & Keenan (1973): reading times correlated very highly with the number of propositions in a sentence, not with the number of words. If that holds, deleting function words (which carry few propositions) cuts words but not propositions, so time per word should rise. — [secondary summary, Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/reading-comprehension-in-polish-and-english/higherlevel-language-processing-discourse-level-and-text-structure-knowledge/93977154CB030B045F4886E7551A6809); [original](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90036-4)
- **[weak, secondary; pop: adults, unfamiliar artificial or foreign language, not L1 reading]** Issidorides (1988): keeping function words did not slow meaning extraction when intonation cues were present, and dropping them did not necessarily speed it up, contradicting the popular belief that omitting them facilitates comprehension. I only saw a catalogue snippet. — [record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edo,ejs14992977)
- **[moderate; pop: agrammatic/non-fluent aphasia]** Telegraphic output is the hallmark of agrammatic production (content words kept, function words and bound morphemes omitted). Comprehension is described as relatively spared but with specific deficits: actives understood, passives near chance; when meaning cannot be recovered from word meanings and world knowledge, comprehension depends on word order, function words and inflection. One account says competent speakers approximate the agrammatic comprehension pattern when given "pruned" strings (I could not verify the original source). I found no study of telegraphic written input as an aid for aphasic readers. — [Aphasiology 2022 abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02687038.2022.2143233); [University of Essex paper](https://publications.essex.ac.uk/esj/article/141/galley/328/download)
- **[moderate; pop: L2 readers]** Elaboration (adding redundancy and explicit relations) beats simplification. Oh (2001, TESOL Quarterly): 180 Korean high-school EFL students, six passages in baseline, simplified or elaborated form, 18-item multiple-choice test; results "support the suggestion that input should be modified in the direction of elaboration rather than" simplification (abstract verified, truncated). Yano, Long & Ross (1994, 13 passages, Japanese EFL learners): no significant score difference between simplified and elaborated versions, elaborated passages were judged more helpful for inference (secondary summary). — [Oh 2001](https://doi.org/10.2307/3587860); [secondary summary of Yano et al.](https://hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2011/9_1-2_LeHong.pdf)

### Inferences
- Verdict: direct experiments on systematic function-word deletion for skilled L1 adults reading ordinary prose appear not to exist in the indexed literature I could reach. Any claim about "caveman style" must be extrapolated from (a) per-word reading-time models, (b) the noisy-channel repair literature, (c) headline-ambiguity work, (d) cohesion/learning studies (Question 4).
- Likely net pattern by outcome (inference, not measured): reading time per word probably falls but time per proposition falls less (Kintsch & Keenan); raw text-level reading time likely drops modestly (Braille result shows direction); comprehension of predictable, low-ambiguity content likely holds because readers already fail to notice most isolated omissions (Staub); comprehension of structurally ambiguous or inference-heavy content likely drops (Perfetti headline ambiguity, Gibson noise-rate effects); subjective preference may diverge from performance in either direction (Eskenazi).
- Gibson et al.'s noise-rate adaptation suggests readers who encounter systematic omission adapt their expectations, so costs may shrink with exposure; no study tested this for telegraphic registers.

### Gaps
- No eye-tracking, self-paced-reading or comprehension experiment on systematically stripped function words in L1 adult prose; no study of subjective effort for such text.
- Unverified: MSU deletion thesis details; Perfetti et al. 1987 results; Issidorides 1988 details; the "pruned strings" aphasia claim; Gibson et al. exact percentages beyond the press summary (PMC full text blocked).
- Nothing found on telegraphic input to aphasic readers (only production descriptions).

## 2. How do skilled readers process function words: are they skipped, and do they still carry parsing value?

### Takeaway
Short function words are skipped far more often than content words, mostly because of word length and parafoveal orthography rather than syntactic fit, but skipping does not mean they are ignored: they still matter for ambiguity resolution, even though readers rarely notice when they are missing or doubled.

### Cited Findings
- **[strong as textbook figures; secondary here]** Content words are fixated about 85% of the time versus about 35% for function words (Rayner 1998 review, as cited in a 2025 Brazilian Portuguese corpus paper); function words that are fixated get fewer and shorter fixations. I did not open the primary review. — [secondary source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/12432282/)
- **[strong; secondary citation of Rayner & McConkie 1976]** Three-letter words are skipped about 67% of the time versus about 20% for 7-8 letter words; about a third of all words are skipped on first pass on average. No source I read reports a skipping rate for "the" alone. — [Rayner, Slattery, Drieghe & Liversedge 2011 (full text)](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/181115/1/Rayner%252C_Slattery%252C_Drieghe%252C_%2526_Liversedge_%25282011%2529.pdf)
- **[moderate]** Word length and contextual predictability have strong, independent effects on skipping probability and on fixation time when not skipped (no interaction); skipping can occur on partial word information. Abstract verified. — [Rayner et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020990)
- **[moderate]** Brysbaert & Drieghe (2003) argued an apparent word-frequency effect on skipping may really reflect word length (secondary description). — [Brysbaert & Drieghe 2003](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/882673/20_Brysbaert_Drieghe_(2003).pdf)
- **[moderate]** Skipping of "the" is largely orthography-driven. In a boundary-paradigm experiment, readers skipped a syntactically illegal "the" preview frequently, "suggesting that, in many cases, they seemed to be unable to detect the syntactic anomaly in the preview", though some evidence of anomaly detection appeared. A later paper summarizes this as readers skipping nearly 50% of words that appeared as "the" even where an article was syntactically impossible (secondary). Chinese parallel: the very frequent particle "de" is skipped on parafoveal orthography, not sentence likelihood. Abstracts verified. — [Angele & Rayner 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029294); [secondary summary](https://typeset.io/pdf/skipping-syntactically-illegal-the-previews-the-role-of-45hv0lgs3l.pdf); [Zang et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2016.1272617)
- **[moderate]** Skipping does not explain missed errors: even when both copies of a repeated "the" were fixated, detection was only 66% (Staub et al. 2019). Proposed mechanism: readers attribute apparent function-word errors to noise in their own eye-movement control. Verified abstract. — [Staub et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-018-1492-z)
- **[moderate; pop: L1 adults]** Optional function words carry parsing/production information that comprehenders use: "that" insertion in object relative clauses is driven by production difficulty, producing a distribution that comprehenders are sensitive to (three experiments). Abstract verified. — [Race & MacDonald 2003, abstract as indexed by OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Race%20MacDonald%20use%20of%20that%20production%20comprehension%20object%20relative%20clauses) (a 2003 Cognitive Science Society conference paper hosted on eScholarship; no stable DOI was returned)
- **[moderate]** Headline brevity underspecifies structure and invites unnoticed ambiguity (see Question 1, Perfetti et al. 1987, partly unverified). — [record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596x(87)90110-0)

### Inferences
- Function-word skipping is cheap and mostly low-level; it implies short function words cost little reading time, so deleting them saves proportionally little time per word (per Rayner 1998-type figures, function words already get about 35% fixation rates and short fixations).
- Missing/duplicated function words are rarely noticed (Staub), which implies they are usually redundant given context (high predictability); the cost of removal should concentrate in the minority of cases where they disambiguate structure (that-clauses, reduced relatives, headline ambiguity).

### Gaps
- Primary skipping rate for "the" specifically was not located (Rayner 1998/2009 reviews not opened).
- Classic garden-path evidence that omitting "that" slows reading of the post-verbal noun phrase (Frazier & Rayner 1982; Trueswell, Tanenhaus & Kello 1993; Garnsey et al. 1997) is not verified in this session; Garnsey et al. 1997 exists (`10.1006/jmla.1997.2512`) but no abstract or numbers were retrieved.
- The Race & MacDonald (2003) citation has no stable DOI or landing page in my retrieval (conference paper; abstract only); verify before quoting.

## 3. What does the Uniform Information Density hypothesis, and work on optional words such as "that", say about ideal information density for comprehenders?

### Takeaway
UID is chiefly a production-side hypothesis supported by corpus data (speakers omit optional markers when the upcoming structure is predictable). Comprehension-side evidence shows reading time is logarithmic in surprisal and that uneven density lowers acceptability, but there is no direct test that a uniform, maximally dense style is easiest to read.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate; corpus; production]** Levy & Jaeger (2007): speakers are more likely to omit an optional relativizer (reduce) in less information-dense phrases, robust across many control variables; speakers use both surface and structural cues to estimate predictability. Abstract verified. — [Levy & Jaeger 2007](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/7503.003.0111)
- **[moderate; corpus; production]** Jaeger (2010, Cognitive Psychology 61): single multilevel logit model on naturally distributed corpus data; "that" in complement clauses is omitted more when the upcoming structure is predictable; information density predicts speaker preferences beyond availability, ambiguity-avoidance and dependency-processing accounts. Not controlled experiments. — [Jaeger 2010 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896231); [bibliographic summary](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/jaeger-redundancyandreductionspeakersmanagesyntacticinformationdensity-2010)
- **[moderate; replication]** Hao & Kaiser (2025, EMNLP): large contemporary conversational corpus plus language-model estimates replicate the information density and that-mentioning link; earlier subcategorization-probability measures capture "substantial idiosyncratic lexical variation", and contextual-embedding estimates explain additional variance. Abstract verified. — [Hao & Kaiser 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.emnlp-main.1117)
- **[strong for the shape of the cost curve]** Reading time rises logarithmically with surprisal over six orders of magnitude of predictability (Smith & Levy 2013); Shain et al. (2024, PNAS) re-examined six reading datasets with diverse language models and nonlinear regression and again support a logarithmic effect, which the authors say favours probabilistic inference as a core component of comprehension. The abstract frames a superlogarithmic effect as the alternative that would reflect a uniform-information pressure; the result favours logarithmic. Abstracts verified. — [Smith & Levy 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2013.02.013); [Shain et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2307876121)
- **[moderate; mixed]** Meister et al. (2021, EMNLP) tested competing UID operationalizations: reading-time data (Natural Stories) consistent with a linear and a mildly superlinear surprisal effect (the latter compatible with UID); acceptability judgments showed clearer evidence that non-uniform information density predicts lower acceptability; the best operationalizations suggest regression toward a language-wide mean surprisal, not a phrase-, sentence- or document-level mean. Secondary summary of abstract. — [Meister et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11635)
- **[moderate]** Wilcox et al. (2023, TACL): surprisal predicts reading times across 11 languages from 5 families; they also tested contextual entropy and the linearity of the linking function (abstract truncated in my retrieval, results beyond the first prediction not verified). — [Wilcox et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00612)

### Inferences
- If processing cost is logarithmic in surprisal, highly predictable function words (low surprisal) cost little, and removing them saves little time while possibly raising surprisal at the next word (less structural certainty); information-dense words (high surprisal) remain the main cost. This predicts modest time savings and some added inference cost for telegraphic text.
- UID's claim that speakers manage density for the comprehender's benefit has not been tested by manipulating density in prose and measuring human comprehension; I found no such experiment.

### Gaps
- No comprehension-side experiment varying overall information density (e.g., omitting/inserting optional words across a text) with reading time, comprehension and effort measured separately.
- Jaeger 2010 effect sizes not extracted (PMC page blocked).

## 4. Text cohesion and connectives: McNamara, Kintsch et al. (1996), the reverse cohesion effect, and replications or failures to replicate

### Takeaway
Explicit cohesion helps low-knowledge and less-skilled readers and, in most studies, text-based recall, while knowledge-rich readers sometimes learn more deeply from sparse text; but the "reverse cohesion effect" depends on reading skill, question type, genre and text, failed to replicate inside the original paper on one measure, and is not a robust general rule.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate; pop: junior high students]** McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996, Cognition & Instruction): Experiment 1 showed advantages for globally coherent and more explanatory biology text; Experiment 2 (varying local and global coherence) found low-knowledge readers benefit from coherent text while high-knowledge readers benefit from a minimally coherent text, explained by compensatory inference processing forced by the poorly written text. Abstract verified. — [McNamara et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1)
- **[moderate; pop: college students]** McNamara & Kintsch (1996, Discourse Processes): high-coherence history text produced better recall and multiple-choice performance (Exp 1); high-knowledge readers did better on a sorting task after the low-coherence text in Exp 1, but "the previous sorting task results failed to replicate" in Exp 2; in Exp 2, high-knowledge readers did better on open-ended questions after the low-coherence text; reading times showed low-coherence text demands more inference processing. Abstract verified. — [McNamara & Kintsch 1996](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539609544975)
- **[moderate; pop: college students, n=143]** O'Reilly & McNamara (2007, Discourse Processes) "Reversing the reverse cohesion effect": the low-cohesion benefit was restricted to less-skilled high-knowledge readers; skilled high-knowledge comprehenders benefited from the high-cohesion text; the cohesion-by-knowledge interaction appeared on text-based questions only; for low-knowledge readers, high cohesion helped bridging-inference questions but not text-based ones. Abstract verified. — [O'Reilly & McNamara 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530709336895)
- **[moderate, secondary]** Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara (2009, Learning & Instruction): reading skill was critical for benefiting from added cohesion, especially among high-knowledge readers (summary only). — [paper record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.04.003)
- **[moderate; pop: grade 4 children]** McNamara, Ozuru & Floyd (2011): children with more knowledge understood low-cohesion narrative texts better (a reverse cohesion pattern) but pattern depended on genre; decoding skill mattered less than prior knowledge for cohesion effects. Abstract verified. — [McNamara et al. 2011](https://doaj.org/article/74d30c243c8c45619d13f7e19fd0b8f6)
- **[moderate]** Kamalski, Sanders & Lentz (2008, Discourse Processes): "this interaction was not consistently found in previous research"; coherence marking by prior knowledge interacted in informative texts but not persuasive ones. Abstract verified. — [Kamalski et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530802145486)
- **[moderate to strong for one study of its kind; pop: 794 Dutch adolescents; outcome: cloze comprehension]** Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders (2019): adding/removing causal, temporal, contrastive and additive connectives in 20 authentic texts: causal and contrastive connectives raised comprehension, additive connectives lowered it; effects consistent across readers (proficiency, education) but not across texts and relation types; connectives affected local comprehension but not global, except a global effect for difficult texts in post hoc analysis. Abstract verified; effect sizes not seen. — [Kleijn et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2019.1605257)
- **[moderate]** Linderholm, Everson, van den Broek & Mischinski (2000): repairing causal structure (temporal order, explicit goals, repairing coherence breaks) helped both more- and less-skilled readers but only for the difficult text, not the easy one. Abstract verified. — [Linderholm et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1804_4)
- **[moderate]** Britton & Gülgöz (1991, J. Educational Psychology) used Kintsch's model to locate where a 1000-word text called for inferences and "repaired" them to produce a principled revision, testing effects on recall and cognitive structures (abstract truncated in my retrieval; effect sizes and direction not read). — [Britton & Gülgöz 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329)
- **[moderate]** Connectives are used incrementally: eye-tracking showed difficulty with diagnostic "because" sentences well before the end of the second clause, contradicting Millis & Just's delayed-integration hypothesis (1994). Abstract verified. — [Traxler, Bybee & Pickering 1997](https://doi.org/10.1080/027249897391982)
- **[weak; pop: adults]** Adults differ in understanding connectives: comprehension depended on lexical transparency (not frequency) and varied with vocabulary size, nonverbal IQ and reasoning style; print exposure did not matter. Abstract verified. — [Scholman, Marchal & Demberg 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853x.2024.2325262)
- **[weak, preprint, not peer reviewed]** An EasyChair preprint revisiting the effect in a scenario-based assessment reports robust prior-knowledge effects, little effect of foundational reading skills, and "no overall effects of the cohesion manipulations on comprehension performance". — [preprint](https://easychair-www.easychair.org/publications/preprint_open/qKMM)
- **[moderate, methodological]** Graesser & McNamara (2010): Coh-Metrix was used to measure what was actually manipulated (cohesion of text base vs situation model, extraneous variables) across published cohesion studies, i.e., manipulations differ across the literature, which can explain inconsistent results. Abstract verified. — [Graesser & McNamara 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2010.01081.x)
- **Meta-analysis status:** I found no meta-analysis isolating connectives or text cohesion. A 2023 Educational Research Review meta-analysis on linguistic features in STEM texts may be relevant but I did not verify its contents.

### Inferences
- Terse text lowers cohesion (connectives and bridging statements dropped), so it should behave like the low-cohesion conditions here: faster to read in raw words but demanding more inference; helpful only for readers with high domain knowledge and strategic skill and mainly on deep/inference measures; harmful for low-knowledge or less-skilled readers (and for difficult material specifically, per Linderholm and Kleijn).
- The benefit of sparse text in this literature is about learning and situation-model quality after effortful processing, not about reading ease. Reading times in the original study (low-coherence text needs more inference) point toward more effort, not less.
- Additive connectives lowering comprehension (Kleijn) shows not every connective is worth keeping; the useful ones are causal and contrastive.

### Gaps
- No primary effect sizes retrieved for McNamara's studies; no large preregistered replication found (only the EasyChair preprint).
- No eye-tracking meta-evidence quantifying how much explicit connectives speed reading (Haberlandt 1982, Millis & Just 1994 not retrieved).

## 5. Does concision improve comprehension and recall? Summaries vs originals, wordiness, seductive details, expertise reversal

### Takeaway
Cutting off-topic or redundant material reliably helps learning and recall when what is cut is truly irrelevant (seductive details, coherence principle: moderate-to-large effects), and focused summaries can beat full chapters; but removing assistance harms novices and helps only experts (expertise reversal d about 0.5 and -0.4), and plain-language redrafting works through simpler words and structure, not deletion alone.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate, abstract relayed by a search summary, not read directly]** Reder & Anderson (1980, J. Verbal Learning & Verbal Behavior): across studies using college textbook chapters and summaries of main points, summaries were better than the full text for retention at 20 minutes, 1 week and 6-12 months, on both directly stated and inference questions, even when main points were underlined in the full text. Reading-time and score numbers not retrieved. — [Reder & Anderson 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(80)90122-x); [citation page](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/reder/publications/80_lmr_jra.html)
- **[strong, secondary figures; pop: mostly students]** Seductive-details meta-analysis (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, Educational Psychology Review): overall effect of including seductive details g about -0.33 (small to moderate, negative), 68 effects from 58 studies, 7,500+ learners (secondary summaries). Moderators are contested: one summary reports larger penalties under fixed pacing and when extras share format with core material; another training-resource summary says format, timing, size, placement and frequency did not change the effect. Rey (2012) is an earlier meta-analysis (not verified here). — [QIC-WD summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf); [ScienceDaily press coverage](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4); [Rey 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003)
- **[moderate to strong; secondary table; pop: multimedia learners, mostly Mayer-lab experiments]** Coherence principle (excluding extraneous material): median Cohen's d 0.86 with 18 of 19 experiments positive (Mayer 2021 table); an older chapter version reported median 1.32 over 10 of 11 experiments. A meta-analysis of 181 multimedia studies lists removing seductive detail among its largest mean effects. Mostly retention/transfer tests on short lessons, not pure reading speed. — [Mayer, The Future of Multimedia Learning](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)
- **[strong; pop: learners from primary to higher education, mostly students]** Expertise reversal meta-analysis (Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025, Learning & Instruction 98): 176 effect sizes, 60 studies, 5,924 participants. Low-prior-knowledge learners learn better from high-assistance instruction (d = 0.505); high-prior-knowledge learners learn better from low-assistance instruction (d = -0.428). Moderated by type of prior-knowledge assessment, educational status and domain; weaker or unclear for younger students and for humanities and language learning; asymmetric (helping novices matters more than withholding from experts). "Assistance" covers worked examples and added explanation broadly, not only wording redundancy. Abstract verified. — [Tetzlaff et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142)
- **[moderate]** A 2023 review of 63 redundancy-effect studies separates content redundancy (overlapping information) from working-memory-channel redundancy, and calls for theoretical consistency in the field. Abstract verified. — [Trypke, Stebner & Wirth 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1148035)
- **[moderate; pop: non-expert adults; outcome: paraphrase and question answering]** Masson & Waldron (1994): three-stage plain-language redrafting of legal contracts (stage 1 removed archaic and redundant terms; stage 2 simplified words and sentence structure; stage 3 defined or replaced legal terms). Comprehension was reliably enhanced by simpler words and sentence structure, but absolute comprehension stayed very low because non-experts struggled with the underlying legal concepts. Abstract verified. — [Masson & Waldron 1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2350080107)
- **[moderate, secondary]** Reading time tracks propositions, not words (Kintsch & Keenan 1973; see Question 1): dense propositional packing slows reading even when word count falls. — [secondary summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/reading-comprehension-in-polish-and-english/higherlevel-language-processing-discourse-level-and-text-structure-knowledge/93977154CB030B045F4886E7551A6809)
- **[weak]** A 2024 scoping review on text redundancy in academic writing reports no shared definition or classification of redundancy and little work specific to academic prose. — [Tikhonova et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.17323/jle.2024.23747)

### Inferences
- "Concision wins" results are about cutting irrelevant or interesting-but-off-topic content (seductive details), or about summaries that foreground main ideas, not about stripping grammatical glue. These are different operations from telegraphic compression and should not be conflated in the report.
- Reder & Anderson plausibly rest on the test matching the summary's content (an inference about design, unverified); summaries give an edge on main-point retention, not necessarily on understanding of details.
- Expertise reversal supports tailoring: terse style should suit experts in the domain, and hurt novices; the effect size for withholding assistance from experts is moderate and smaller than the harm to novices.

### Gaps
- Primary effect sizes for Reder & Anderson, Sundararajan & Adesope and Rey 2012 were not read directly; the moderator conflict on seductive details is unresolved.
- No study found on wordiness or padding per se (words that are redundant restatements) with reading time, comprehension and effort separated.

## 6. Extremely concise technical styles in practice: Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100), controlled languages, checklists, military brevity, clinical shorthand

### Takeaway
The few experiments on Simplified English show comprehension gains for complex documents and for non-native readers, no reading-time gain, and no test of SE against good conventional technical writing; clinical abbreviation shorthand, in contrast, shows poor cross-reader comprehension. Nothing measuring checklists or military brevity codes as reading styles was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate, one lab; pop: 130 engineering undergraduates, native and non-native]** Shubert, Spyridakis, Holmback & Coney (1995, J. Technical Writing & Communication) and its conference version: two real Boeing maintenance procedures, each in SE and pre-1990 non-SE form; 20 two-part questions (comprehension plus content-location identification), 30-minute limit. SE readers of the more complex Procedure A comprehended significantly more and located content significantly better, for native and non-native readers (non-natives benefited most; native/non-native contrast could not be tested statistically because of unequal cell sizes); no significant effect for the simpler Procedure B; SE and non-SE took "essentially the same" time to read and test. Abstract verified; full results read in the authors' 1996 summary. — [Shubert et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.2190/wg69-d74b-4dll-2wbk); [Holmback, Shubert & Spyridakis 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Holmback.pdf)
- **[moderate; pop: aircraft maintenance technicians; secondary via authors' summary]** Chervak, Drury & Ouellette (1996, FAA report): for the two easy workcards there was no significant accuracy change between SE and non-SE; for the two difficult workcards "Simplified English gave clearly superior accuracy"; very few non-native speakers but their performance was much better with SE; reading ability correlated moderately with comprehension. — [Holmback et al. 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Holmback.pdf)
- **[weak; pop: 15 Spanish, 17 Chinese, 6 Japanese speakers translating]** Translatability of SE: across three languages SE versions got higher style-match ratings and fewer minor omissions; for Spanish speakers also higher accuracy and comprehensibility and fewer minor mistranslations; no significant differences for Chinese; Japanese too few to analyze. Authors flag small cell sizes. — [Holmback et al. 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Holmback.pdf)
- **[methodological caveats from the authors]** There is no SE compliance standard; the authors used naturally occurring documents (SE versions post-1990, non-SE versions pre-1990); the two procedures differed in complexity; no time pressure was applied so speed claims were untested; they did not compare SE with the best conventional technical writing; subjects were first-time SE readers; tests focused on the SE-motivated differences between versions. — [Holmback et al. 1996](https://aclanthology.org/www.mt-archive.info/90/CLAW-1996-Holmback.pdf)
- **[weak]** Stewart (2009, University of British Columbia): 41 non-native English electronics-technician students, SE vs non-SE aircraft procedure, with English reading level as a factor; the abstract I retrieved was truncated before results. — [Stewart 2009](https://doi.org/10.14288/1.0078187)
- **[moderate; survey, pop: clinicians]** Hamiel et al. (2018, Postgraduate Medical Journal): only 1.2% of physicians could understand 50% or more of the abbreviations in ophthalmologists' notes (secondary summary). Jayatilake & Oyibo (2023, Cureus): 20 abbreviations from real records given to 46 volunteers (15 nurses, 15 doctors in training, 16 consultants); the proportion giving the intended meaning ranged from 0 to 87% depending on the abbreviation. — [Cureus 2023 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10479966); [PSNet record of an abbreviation-comprehension study](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/frequency-comprehension-and-attitudes-physicians-towards-abbreviations-medical-record)
- **[weak, secondary news-level figures, not verified]** Reports that paediatricians agreed on abbreviation meaning 56-94% of the time versus 31-63% for other professionals, and that 5% of 30,000 medication errors were linked to abbreviations, came from a UK news article summary and were not traced to primary studies. — [news-level summary](https://hospitalhealthcare.com/?p=2378)

### Inferences
- SE is not telegraphic style: it restricts vocabulary and sentence structure to reduce ambiguity, and the evidence that it helps is concentrated in complex, procedural text and non-native readers; null for speed. It is therefore weak evidence for or against dropping function words.
- Clinical shorthand is idiosyncratic and domain-specific, so its cost is about decoding shared meaning, which is a different mechanism from reading terse but conventional grammar.
- The pattern "benefit grows with complexity" (SE studies) parallels the cohesion results (repairs help difficult texts) and expertise reversal, consistent with a general moderator: text difficulty and reader knowledge.

### Gaps
- No studies retrieved on checklists as a reading format, military brevity codes or aviation phraseology (spoken and written), or the Kuhn (2014) survey of controlled natural languages; nothing on ASD-STE100's article-retention rule (not verified).
- No eye-tracking or reading-speed study of SE under time pressure; none comparing SE to good non-SE writing.

## 7. Recent work (2023 to 2026) on how humans read compressed or terse LLM-produced text, and prompt/output compression styles

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study of human comprehension of telegraphic or "caveman"-style LLM output was found. A handful of 2025-2026 arXiv preprints give indirect signals: compression is evaluated mainly for machine readers, simplification helps humans modestly, and the one human test of aggressively compressed text shows lower accuracy with unreported magnitude.

### Cited Findings
- **[weak, preprint; pop: 4,563 participants; outcome: multiple-choice accuracy and self-reported ease]** Guidroz et al. (2025, arXiv 2505.01980): randomized study with 31 texts across six domains; LLM-simplified text (self-refinement, minimal information loss) raised accuracy by 3.9 percentage points (p < 0.05); domain gains PubMed 14.6%, finance 5.5%, aerospace/CS 3.8%, legal 3.5%; gain persisted with or without access to the text; perceived ease higher by 0.33 on a 5-point simplified NASA-TLX scale (p < 0.05). Simplification, not telegraphic compression. — [Guidroz et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980)
- **[weak, preprint; pop: 24 professionals]** Mountford et al. (2026, arXiv 2603.06878): participants judged whether LLM reasoning outputs (42 to 150 words, tertiles short/medium/long) on 15 Watson-Glaser items were correct. Probability of correct judgment, incorrect outputs: short 24.5% [12.7, 42.2], medium 54.2% [36.2, 71.3], long 30.7% [16.9, 49.1]; correct outputs: 71.5%, 81.1%, 79.5%. Length mattered mainly when the LLM was wrong; medium length was best; confidence intervals overlap; reading time and cognitive load were not measured; one LLM (GPT-5 default, August 2025); natural length variation confounded with other features. — [Mountford et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06878v1)
- **[weak, preprint; extreme compression for machine readers]** Zhu et al. (2026, arXiv 2606.19857) "BabelTele": text condensed to 27.9% of original length retains "99.5% semantic fidelity" for instruction-tuned LLM readers. A human questionnaire (paid university students, four-option QuALITY questions, about 10 passages with 3 questions each in the section I read) reports "a QA accuracy drop on BabelTele inputs", but the magnitude appears only in a figure and the participant count was not stated; BabelTele Dale-Chall 16.70, difficult-word ratio 80.19%. This is deliberately human-unreadable machine-oriented text, so it bounds the far end of compression rather than testing telegraphic English. — [Zhu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19857)
- **[weak, preprint, machine-side only]** Arbuzov et al. (2026, arXiv 2605.04426) "Telegraph English": structured symbolic rewriting for prompt compression; a search summary reports roughly 50% token reduction with 99.1% key-fact accuracy on GPT-4.1 and no human readability measure; I could not read the abstract or results in the PDF. — [Arbuzov et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.04426)
- **[weak, preprint; LLM reasoning not human reading]** Chain of Draft (Xu et al. 2025): matches or exceeds chain-of-thought accuracy using as little as 7.6% of the tokens (best case), with roughly five words per reasoning step as a guideline; tested with models such as Claude 3.5 Sonnet. — [Chain of Draft](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600v1)
- **[anecdotal / very weak, preprint with no figures]** Esposito (2025, arXiv 2508.04713) claims users "overwhelmingly prefer concise, source-attributed responses" across five AI search systems and that verbosity raises cognitive load; the abstract cites "10.000 (est.) participants", reports no numbers or statistics and no venue. Preference, not comprehension. — [Esposito 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04713)
- **[anecdotal]** A PromptZone post describes telegraphese prompts cutting tokens on summarization while hurting readability for non-experts, with no methodology. — [PromptZone](https://www.promptzone.com/deepa_kowalski/llms-relearn-telegraphese-in-1866-style-ehk)

### Inferences
- The LLM-compression literature optimizes tokens and model accuracy; human comprehension is rarely measured, and when it is (BabelTele), it drops. That is consistent with the neighbouring evidence that machine-recoverable and human-recoverable meaning diverge, but magnitudes are unknown.
- Mountford et al. suggest a non-monotonic relation between output length and error detection; very short outputs may hide reasoning errors from human checkers. This is tentative (24 participants, overlapping CIs).
- Human-reader studies of LLM output that isolate style (telegraphic vs full) from content and length are a clear gap in 2023-2026 work.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed human-subject study of terse/telegraphic LLM answers (reading time, comprehension, effort); no study of the "caveman" prompt style specifically.
- Known length-bias literature on human preference for long LLM answers (e.g., RLHF length correlation) was not retrieved and is not covered here.
- Participant counts and magnitudes for the BabelTele human test, and the Telegraph English abstract, were not read.
