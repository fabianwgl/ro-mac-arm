# Visual format, structure and layout of written text: effects on reading speed, accuracy, comprehension and effort

Evidence-strength tags used below: **[MA]** meta-analysis or systematic review; **[Overview]** meta-meta-analysis / overview of reviews; **[Rep]** several experiments, mixed or replicated; **[Single]** single experiment or small set from one lab; **[Industry]** non-peer-reviewed industry research; **[Snippet]** only an abstract or search-result summary was seen (primary not opened). Population tags: **[adults]**, **[students]**, **[children]**, **[dyslexia]**, **[low vision]**. Research coverage runs to November 2026, but searches turned up little after 2023 apart from the items cited. Tool limitations (publisher paywalls, 403s) meant many primary papers were read only at abstract level; each such case is flagged.

## Bullet lists and numbered lists versus continuous prose

### Takeaway
There is no meta-analysis of lists versus prose. The small controlled literature says lists help recall and comprehension of discrete, parallel items (medication instructions, procedures, health flyers, recipes) but the benefit shrinks when items are heterogeneous, and bullets can reduce recall of the surrounding running text. Evidence on search speed and perceived effort is almost absent.

### Cited Findings
- [Rep, adults, young and old] Morrow et al. (1998): list-format medication instructions were understood (answer time and accuracy) and recalled better than paragraph format for younger and older adults, and lists reduced age gaps. Category headers did not impair comprehension but did reduce recall. — [Duke repository record](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/4ee813b1-7046-4e08-83c5-5b6d9861be60) [Snippet]
- [Rep, adults] Jansen (2014, Information Design Journal 21(2), 146-162), five studies of everyday functional texts (health flyers, direct-mail letters, recipes), bulleted lists vs formatted paragraphs: bullets improved recall of the listed items in 3 studies and improved readers' evaluation of the text in 2 studies; the recall benefit was mitigated when the listed components were heterogeneous; in 2 studies bullets worsened recall of content in the surrounding text. — [Benjamins abstract](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan) [Snippet, abstract only; effect sizes not seen]
- [Rep, adults] Two experiments found recall was better for lists arranged as separate items than for lists run on continuously; the authors attribute this to separate layout letting readers re-read selectively instead of sequentially. — [Visible Language, "Using Lists to Improve Text Access"](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5702) [Snippet; authors/year not confirmed]
- [Single, engineering students n=110] Garner & Alley's assertion-evidence slide study: slides built on six multimedia principles (full-sentence assertion headline plus visual evidence, no default PowerPoint bullet hierarchy) vs default bullet-style slides gave superior essay comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger delayed recall. Confound: six principles changed at once, so bullets alone are not isolated; it is also a slide, not document, context. — [OpenAlex record (SciComm Journal Club summary of Garner & Alley)](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Garner+Alley+assertion-evidence+slides+audience+comprehension) [Snippet]
- [Rep, adults, procedural text] A study of procedural text found rereading raised recall of list-like instructions more than narrative instructions. — search result "The Effect of Structure on the Memory for Procedural Text" (via [Springer BF03186769](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03186769)) [Snippet]
- Related displays: flowcharts for procedural instructions beat standard paragraphs in comprehension accuracy and speed in a 1975 Human Factors study, while a Dutch review reports flowcharts are remembered worse than prose. — [scilit listing](https://scilit.com/publications/4d379c6514788c30e82a50ea020c16e3) and [Tijdschrift voor Taalbeheersing](https://dbnl.org/tekst/_tij009199001_01/_tij009199001_01_0002.php) [Snippet, conflicting]

### Inferences
- The mechanism consistent with the evidence: lists externalize parallel structure and give discrete retrieval cues, helping recall of items and quick scanning; they strip connectives ("because", "unless", "therefore"), so they should suit parallel, homogeneous items and fit poorly when ideas stand in causal or conditional relations. Jansen's heterogeneity moderator and the surrounding-text recall cost are the only direct evidence for this "lost relations" concern; it is a plausible but thinly tested extrapolation.
- Garner & Alley is evidence for "headline assertion plus visual" over "topic title plus bullet fragments", not for or against bullets in running documents.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis or large preregistered study on bullets vs prose was found. No study found that measured search/lookup speed or subjective effort for lists vs prose in documents. Effect sizes were not available for Morrow et al. or Jansen. No study found directly testing "relations between ideas lost" with inference questions.

## Headings, signalling, topic sentences, overviews and summaries

### Takeaway
Signalling has a consistent small-to-moderate positive effect in multimedia/learning meta-analyses (g about 0.3 to 0.5 on learning, about 0.25 on subjective cognitive load), with the biggest benefit for low-prior-knowledge learners and complex material. For headings specifically (Lorch programme), free recall and topic-search speed usually improve, recognition tests usually show no effect, and results are not universal.

### Cited Findings
- [Overview] Noetel et al. (2022, Review of Educational Research): 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants. Signaling is among the largest benefits; for signaling, learning g = 0.43 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.50, k = 209) and cognitive load g = 0.25 (CI 0.04 to 0.45, k = 27) from Schneider et al. (2018); learning-relevant fixations g = 0.39; motivation g = 0.13. Noetel defines signals as cues such as arrows, highlighted text, laser pointer. — [Noetel et al. abstract](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8zqy4/multimedia-design-for-learning-an-overview-of-reviews-with-meta-meta-analysis); full text read at [PDF copy](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [MA] Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018, Educational Research Review 23): Noetel Table 1 reports "transfer g = 0.53, retention g = 0.33" (table columns are garbled in the text extraction) while a French lecture deck reports retention 0.53 (139 studies, CI 0.42-0.64), transfer 0.33 (70 studies, CI 0.22-0.43), cognitive load 0.25, motivation 0.13, learning duration -0.30. **The two secondary sources disagree on which outcome is 0.53 and which 0.33**; the primary abstract was not retrievable. Noetel also notes text signalling generally helped retention and transfer more than graphic signalling, with consistent effects across education levels, disciplines, prior knowledge, and pacing. — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true); [Univ. Grenoble slides](https://inspe-sciedu.gricad-pages.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/slides/UE-24-option-rech-5.pdf); article at [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001)
- [MA] Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020, ETR&D): signalling in multimedia learning d = 0.38; larger for multimedia (d = 0.63), low-prior-knowledge learners (d = 0.47), dynamic signals (d = 0.43). — [Noetel Table 1 summary](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true); [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7)
- [MA] Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016, Educational Research Review 17): signalling text-picture relations, 27 studies / 45 comparisons / 2,464 participants, r = 0.17 (95% CI .11-.22) on transfer/comprehension; low-prior-knowledge r = 0.19, high-prior-knowledge r = -0.08. — [search record](https://core.ac.uk/download/84109201.pdf) and [Noetel Table 1](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [Overview, caution] Noetel: signaling's cognitive-load effect in another review (Xie et al., 2017) was small, g = 0.11 (CI 0.04-0.18, k = 32); Noetel's Table 1 also lists a review (probably the same Xie et al. 2017; attribution not certain in the extracted text) with d = 0.27 retention, 0.34 transfer, -0.11 cognitive load (negative = lower load). So the 0.25 cognitive-load figure is the upper end. Across the 29 reviews only 2 reported duplicate screening and only 1 a formal study-quality assessment; 20/29 assessed publication bias. — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [MA, students] Ponce, Mayer & Méndez (2022, Educational Psychology Review): highlighting meta-analysis, 36 articles (1938-2019), 85 effect sizes. Instructor-provided highlighting helped college (0.41) and school (0.48) learners; learner-generated highlighting 0.39 in college, 0.24 in school; another abstract version reports learner-generated highlighting improved memory (0.36) but not comprehension (0.20). Metric not stated in snippets. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1) [Snippet]
- [Rep, adults, Lorch programme; review not meta-analysis] Lemarié, Lorch & Péry-Woodley (2012, Discours): free recall usually benefits from headings (Holley 1981; Krug 1989; Lorch & Lorch 1996a; Sanchez, Lorch & Lorch 2001), but "headings do not always lead to improved free recall" (Lorch & Lorch 1996b); recognition tests usually show no effect (Spyridakis studies), attributed to recognition's insensitivity to organization. Headings also usually aid summarization (Brooks 1983; Hyönä & Lorch 2004; others). The review reports no effect sizes. — [Discours 8600](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600)
- [Rep, adults] Headings steer attention to topics and build a more systematic representation of topic structure; when a heading repeats a topic sentence readers attend less to that sentence yet memory improves (Hyönä & Lorch 2004, eye-fixation study; Lorch, Lorch & Inman 1993). — [Discours review](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600); [Hyönä & Lorch 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.01.001)
- [Rep, adults, search] Lorch, Lemarié & Grant (2011b): when questions targeted specific sentences, topic-identifying headings produced much faster search than control texts; sequential numbering gave faster search than hierarchical numbering, but hierarchical was better for outlining (2011a). — [Discours review](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600)
- [Rep] Moderators: signals matter more as text organization is more complex or harder to perceive; they are ignored when they do not serve reader goals (Golding & Fowler 1992); simple topic structure showed no signalling effect in a free-recall study with headings, overviews and summaries. — [Lorch & Lemarié chapter](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-39194-1_43.pdf) and [SARA/Discours snippets](https://doaj.org/article/b268e54e7dcb49e7b7daa223464636d6) [Snippet]
- [Theory] SARA (Lemarié, Lorch, Eyrolle & Virbel 2008, Educational Psychologist 43: 27-48): profiles each signal by scope, realization, location and information function (seven information types). The Lorch group argues "do headings help?" is the wrong question; effects depend on function. Also, heading-vs-no-heading comparisons confound organization with white space/demarcation; a row of asterisks alone made readers rate the following section's statements more important. — [SARA record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c497t-f27caee38683d3853645748b95c8c10b49cfb0e845f5a7efa690d825722fe1f03); [Discours review](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600)
- [Single, unpublished] Chen & Lorch (in preparation): under time pressure, participants recalled more from sections headed "summary". — [Discours review](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600) [treat as provisional]
- [MA, students] Advance organizers: Mahar's (1992) update of 30 years of studies: experimental groups with organizers outperformed 67% of control participants; 56% of post-1980 organizers lacked the two features most associated with effect size (linking familiar to unfamiliar material). The earlier Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980) meta-analysis concluded organizers "as constructed" often did not help; Stone (1983) found help for all ability levels. — [Illinois IDEALS record](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405); [Kabarak journal summary](https://journals.kabarak.ac.ke/index.php/kjri/article/download/129/128/127) [Snippet]

### Inferences
- Signalling effects are in the range g about 0.3 to 0.5 for learning from multimedia/instructional material in lab studies, shrinking once publication bias and heterogeneity are accounted for (smaller cognitive-load effect in Xie et al. 2017). For ordinary prose with headings the best evidence is the Lorch line (recall, summarization, search speed), which gives qualitative but not pooled effects.
- r = 0.17 converts to roughly d = 0.35 (my conversion), consistent with the other meta-analytic estimates; Mahar's "67th percentile" corresponds to roughly d = 0.44 (my conversion).
- Effect is conditional: bigger for novices, complex or poorly organised text; near zero where structure is simple or the test is recognition.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specific to headings in text-only documents was found; the Noetel/Schneider/Alpizar estimates mostly involve multimedia signals (arrows, colour, highlighting) and so extrapolate to headings only partly.
- Meyer's (structure strategy / top-level structure) work and Mayer's own signalling summary (median d, count of tests) were not retrieved. No controlled study isolating topic sentences as such was found (only the Hyönä & Lorch finding that headings duplicating topic sentences shift attention). Hartley & Trueman (1983, headings and recall/search) was referenced via a slide deck only; its claims (headings in either statement or question form helped recall, search, and retrieval for 14-15-year-olds) are unverified. — [slide deck](https://www.slideshare.net/KaylaJones85/a-research-strategy-for-text-desigbers-the-role-of-headingspdf)
- Primary Schneider et al. abstract not retrievable; retention/transfer values conflict between secondary sources.

## Order of information: conclusion first versus build-up

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin and mixed. The few experiments on news structure find inverted-pyramid (conclusion-first) text no better, and sometimes worse, for recall and comprehension than chronological/narrative structure, though readers may prefer it and it may take less processing effort. No controlled study of "bottom line up front" in business or technical documents was found.

### Cited Findings
- [Single, TV news, n=60] Spanish experiment: the inverted pyramid was the least effective narrative structure for information gain; chronological narration improved recall and understanding. — [UCM record](https://eprints.ucm.es/id/eprint/65611/) [Snippet]
- [Single, written news] Missouri dissertation (2008): inverted-pyramid vs chronological stories: no differences in memory or enjoyment, comprehension marginally favored the inverted pyramid, and secondary-task reaction times were slower for chronological stories (more meaning construction). — [MOspace](https://mospace.umsystem.edu/items/4f5c3c35-b2a9-4339-bc2f-4672afe32f88) [Snippet]
- [Single, online news with video, 2009] Recognition of story details was more accurate for narrative than inverted-pyramid versions; the inverted pyramid appeared to need more cognitive resources for encoding the related video. — [JOBEM record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/08838150903333023) [Snippet]
- [Single, online news across age groups, 2017] Narrative articles left people better informed, but appreciation was lower than for inverted-pyramid stories. — [Journalism Studies record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1461670X.2017.1324316) [Snippet]
- Overall, none of these measured total reading time directly; the St. Petersburg Times readership claim in a trade blog is unverified. — [blog summary](https://wyliecomm.com/2020/08/inverted-pyramid-structure-of-news-writing)
- Advance organizers and overviews (conclusion/overview before detail) have mixed-to-small positive meta-analytic support for students, with large variation (see previous section: Mahar 67th percentile; Luiten 1980 mostly non-significant). — [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405) [MA, Snippet]
- Scanning-oriented web writing: NN/g's directive that the opening two paragraphs should carry key points rests on heatmap observation that readers engage more with the first than the second paragraph; no statistics reported. — [NN/g F-pattern article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/) [Industry]

### Inferences
- "Conclusion first" is best supported as a way to let scanners and time-pressed readers stop early, not as a comprehension booster for committed readers; for readers who read to the end, narrative/chronological order may aid recall of events. Which one wins likely depends on genre (news events vs. decisions/instructions) and on whether the reader stops reading.
- The lack of any BLUF test in organizational writing means the practice is currently supported by theory (signalling, advance organizers) and industry practice, not by direct experiments.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of conclusion-first vs build-up in memos, emails, reports or technical documents; no study measuring reading time or subjective effort for inverted pyramid. Full texts of the news experiments (sample sizes, effect sizes) were not opened. Kintsch/Kieras-style main-idea-first research was not retrieved.

## Chunking, paragraph length, tables versus prose, and text plus diagrams (Mayer, Sweller)

### Takeaway
Paragraph length per se has almost no controlled evidence. For multimedia, the cognitive-load/Mayer principles show moderate meta-analytic support (pooled g about 0.38 across principles), with the biggest effects for contiguity (integrating labels/text with the diagram) and signalling, but with a patchy replication record, strong dependence on complex material and system-paced delivery, and publication-bias shrinkage for some principles. Tables beat prose for comparison/data lookup in the few pre-registered and classic studies available.

### Cited Findings
**Paragraphs and chunking**
- [Rep, mixed] Evidence on segmentation cues in text is inconsistent: some studies find no comprehension or recall benefit from paragraph-level cues; Aaronson & Ferres (1983) concluded chunking mainly matters for recall/memorization tasks, not comprehension; indentation vs spacing as paragraph cue showed no significant difference (1979); one study found segmenting idea units had no effect in simple read-and-recall. — [search summary links](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/5377/4241/7054), [Cognitive Psychology Korea](https://accesson.kr/cogpsych/v.2/0/45/24883?lang=en) [Snippet]
- [MA, multimedia lessons] Segmenting a multimedia lesson into learner-paced chunks: retention g about 0.32, transfer g about 0.36 and cognitive load g about 0.23; Rey et al. (2019): g = 0.34 (k = 123); instructor-segmented 0.41 vs learner-segmented 0.20; segmented lessons took longer to complete (g = 0.92). Noetel's authors suggest this may extend to subheadings or short chapters in written material; this extension is theirs, not tested. — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [MA] Removing seductive (interesting but irrelevant) details improves learning g = 0.33 (CI 0.18-0.48, k = 68; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020), larger for persistently on-screen details (0.43) and novices (0.52). — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)

**Tables vs prose**
- [Single, pre-registered, n=2,305] Brick, McDowell & Freeman (2020, Royal Society Open Science): UK census-matched sample, longitudinal online experiment, comprehension of harms and benefits: a table ("fact box") improved comprehension relative to text-only (d reported as 0.39 in a secondary summary) and six-week recall (d = 0.12); no difference in decisions, feeling informed or trust; fact boxes were rated more engaging; pattern held across numeracy and education. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.190876) (abstract); d = 0.39 value from a [search summary](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.190876) [Single, adults]
- [Rep, students] Kiewra et al.: with identical content, matrices and outlines beat plain text for relational learning, and matrices beat outlines; matrix localizes related information within topics and categories. Passages were short (fish, wildcats), college students. — [Kiewra, Kauffman, Robinson & DuBois 1999](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00892032); [Kiewra lab PDF](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf) [Snippet]
- A study of tabulated vs prose data found tabulated data generally better for recalling data values and covariation (authors/year not given in snippet); an older study found tabular treatments raised comprehension and graph+table combinations gave slower but more accurate performance. — [scilit "Learning from Tables"](https://scilit.com/publications/2ad67a7d86552e7ae11ee2aab33efa5a), [UMD LCCD record](https://lccd.umiacs.umd.edu/node/16968) [Snippet]

**Text plus diagrams, cognitive load theory, Mayer principles**
- [Overview] Noetel et al.: the 11 largest reviews pooled 808 effects from 66,553 participants: average design-principle effect on learning g = 0.38 (CI 0.27-0.49); retention g = 0.42 and transfer g = 0.43; cognitive load management g = 0.22 (CI 0.04-0.40, k = 68). Effects were larger for system-paced (lecture-like, g = 0.41) than learner-paced (website-like, g = 0.27) delivery and for high-element-interactivity (complex) material (g = 0.70) than simple (g = 0.20); prior knowledge, education level, medium (paper vs computer) and domain did not significantly moderate. Their conclusion: results support many tenets of both cognitive load theory and the cognitive theory of multimedia learning. — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [MA] Contiguity / split attention (text far from the diagram it describes): Ginns (2006) overall d = 0.85, spatial d = 0.72, temporal d = 0.78, bigger for high-element-interactivity material (d = 0.78) than simple (d = 0.28); Schroeder & Cenkci (2018): g = 0.63 (CI 0.55-0.71, k = 58, n = 2,426). — [Noetel Table 1 and text](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true); [Schroeder & Cenkci](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9)
- [Non-replication example, students n=146] Zhang, de Koning & Paas (2024, Applied Cognitive Psychology): no split-attention effect on retention, comprehension or extraneous load ratings, and no interaction with presentation size. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.4223)
- [MA] Modality effect (spoken instead of printed text with visuals): g = 0.38 but about 0.20 after correcting for publication bias; eliminated when self-paced. Verbal redundancy (adding on-screen words to audio): g = 0.15 (g = 0.29 when adding key words to audio, -0.04 when adding audio to text). — [Noetel PDF](https://files.constantcontact.com/1145f86e901/7bc63268-3738-46ae-9ee0-fbdafb58537f.pdf?rdr=true)
- [MA, students] Adding illustrations to text: Lei et al. (2025) pooled 63 studies / 121 effects / 7,621 students on science achievement, g = 0.232 (CI 0.166-0.298), larger at higher grades and for understanding/transfer tests; paper vs computer did not moderate. Earlier reviews: Levie & Lentz (1982, 55 experiments) and Carney & Levin (2002) found well-designed illustrations improve learning from text; another meta-analysis (39 studies, 1985-2018) noted graphics sometimes had no effect on comprehension. — [Lei et al. record](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/effects-of-adding-illustrations-to-texts-on-students-science-achi/); [Carney & Levin 2002](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02765184) [Snippet]
- [MA] Subjective cognitive-load questionnaires used in this literature have satisfactory internal consistency, and correlations with learning variables support cognitive load theory's three-factor model (Krieglstein et al. 2022); correlations among the load types partly contradict the theory. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-022-09683-4)
- [Children, MA 2025] Interactive features in children's digital picture books had effect sizes close to zero on story comprehension; mini-games hurt, actions mirroring the story's protagonist helped. — [Bus et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10409289.2025.2571978)

### Inferences
- Applied to document design: put each label/legend next to the thing it describes, keep irrelevant embellishment out, break long sequences into meaningful learner-controlled chunks, and use tables when the reader must compare along dimensions. These follow from medium-sized lab effects, mostly with students and short multimedia materials, so expected gains in professional reading are likely smaller than the headline g values.
- The larger effect in complex, system-paced materials and near-zero effect for self-paced simple material suggests layout/design changes matter most when load is high and the reader cannot control pace.

### Gaps
- No empirical study located on paragraph length (sentences per paragraph) as an independent variable for skilled adult readers. The "Mayer multimedia principle" headline figure (median d about 1.4) could not be verified in any retrieved source and is not used here. A direct pre-registered large-scale replication of core Mayer/Sweller principles was not found. Tables-vs-prose evidence for ordinary professional documents (not medical risk or short lab passages) is thin; Wright & Reid (1973, J Appl Psychol 57: 160-166) on contingency tables vs prose was found only as a citation. — [scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/4d379c6514788c30e82a50ea020c16e3)

## Typography and layout; screen versus paper

### Takeaway
Robust: the screen-inferiority effect is small and shrinking in newer work (paper advantage g about -0.2 to -0.25 for older computer-based studies, about -0.1 for handheld devices), concentrated in informational text and time-limited reading, with reading time not reliably different. Typeface category (serif/sans) has essentially no reliable effect, no single type size or font is best for everyone, line length around 55 characters per line appears to optimize comprehension while longer lines read faster on screen, and dark-on-light is advantageous in ordinary lighting. Dyslexia-specific fonts do not help; extra spacing might.

### Cited Findings
**Screen versus paper**
- [MA, adults and students] Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmerón (2018, Educational Research Review 25: 23-38): 54 comparisons (38 between-participant, 16 within), 171,055 participants, 2000-2017, Hedges g = -0.21 for both designs favoring paper. Three significant moderators: time frame (paper advantage larger under time-constrained than self-paced reading), text genre (advantage for informational or mixed texts, not narrative-only), publication year (advantage grew over years). — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003)
- [MA] Clinton (2019, Journal of Research in Reading): 33 randomized studies, n = 2,799; reading performance g = -0.25 (screen worse); expository g = -0.32, narrative g = -0.04; literal g = -0.33, inferential g = -0.26; reading time g = 0.08 (not reliable, 14 studies, n = 1,233); calibration better on paper g = 0.20 (11 studies, n = 698). Results similar for adults and children. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269)
- [MA, children/adolescents/students] Salmerón, Altamura, Delgado & Karagiorgi (2023, Journal of Educational Psychology): handheld devices vs print, between-participant (k = 38) g = -0.113, within-participant (k = 21) g = -0.103; inferiority higher for undergraduates than younger students and for individually tested groups. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000830)
- [Industry/secondary] A blog claims Clinton later ran experiments that failed to find the screen effect; not verified. — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/screen-reading-worse-for-comprehension-leads-to-overconfidence-new-meta-analysis-concludes/2019/04) [Snippet, unverified]

**Reading speed and speed-reading**
- [MA/review, adults] Rayner et al. (2016, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 17: 4-34): reading faster generally costs comprehension; skilled readers average about 200-400 wpm; doubling or tripling speed with intact comprehension is unlikely; skimming gets through text faster with moderate comprehension; apparent speed readers usually have prior knowledge. — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/speed-reading-promises-are-too-good-to-be-true-scientists-find-2)

**Systematic review of typography**
- [MA/systematic review] Ho Sang & Petrarca (2025, Visible Language 59(3)): 42 peer-reviewed empirical studies (2000-2025, Latin script). Key findings: serifs are not a significant legibility factor (just over half of 19 studies comparing serif vs sans found a null effect, with some studies favoring each side); no single type size or typeface optimizes readability for everyone; familiarity may matter. Type size was the most studied variable (n = 18), then line length (n = 9), line spacing (n = 8), letter spacing (n = 6), paragraph spacing (n = 4), text alignment (n = 1), word spacing (n = 2). — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)

**Type size, line spacing, spacing**
- [Rep, adults, low vision] Within that review: Bernard et al. (2003) found no effect of type size on reading speed for young adults; Soleimani & Mohammadi (2012; 120 students) found 12 pt read faster than 10 pt but no effect on comprehension or recall, and no effect of font style or line spacing on speed or comprehension; Sheedy et al. (2005) found performance rose up to 10 pt; smaller sizes hurt glance reading (Dobres et al. 2018) and readers with vision loss (Kanonidou et al. 2014); one study found smaller type, smaller leading and positional uncertainty hurt readability, but additional leading did not rescue small text. — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)
- [Rep] Increased letter spacing beyond normal can impair word recognition and reading eye movements for typical readers (Schneps et al. 2013; Slattery & Rayner 2013; Risko et al. 2011; Beier & Oderkerk 2019 found tighter-than-normal spacing slowed reading), though extra spacing helped weaker readers nearly catch up. — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)
- [Rep, justification] No controlled justified-vs-ragged study was retrieved. Only one study of text alignment (n = 1) is in the systematic review; accessibility guidance (rivers of white space for some readers with reading disabilities) is guideline-level, not experimental. — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208); [WWU accessibility guide](https://marcom.wwu.edu/accessibility/guide/ensure-typography-readable-and-customizable?page=1)

**Line length**
- [Rep, adults] Dyson & Haselgrove (2001): medium line length of about 55 characters gave best comprehension at normal and fast reading speeds and was read faster than shorter lines; later summaries report about 100 characters per line fastest on screen while readers prefer 45-72 characters; correcting oculomotor return-sweep errors did not raise speed; results across studies are inconsistent because tasks and line lengths differ; Nanavati & Bias (2005) review recommends line length not exceed about 70 characters; an online news study found 95 characters per line read faster. — [Dyson summaries](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html); [Nanavati & Bias review](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5765); [ERAU online-news study](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-line-length-on-reading-performance-of-online-news-/) [Snippet]
- [Single, search task] Ling & van Schaik (2006), as summarized in the Ho Sang review: longer lines gave faster searches but reduced accuracy; typeface had no effect; participants preferred shorter lines and Arial. — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)

**Typeface and individual differences**
- [Single, large online sample, adults] Wallace et al. (2022, ACM TOCHI): the best vs worst font for each individual could differ by about 35% in reading speed with comprehension maintained; no single factor predicted speed, and stated font preference did not predict speed even with size controlled. An earlier version used 386 crowdsourced skilled readers (18-71) and 16 fonts. — [Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/towards-individuated-reading-experiences-different-fonts-increase-reading-speed-for-different-individuals); [TOCHI PDF](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)

**Contrast / polarity**
- [Rep, adults young and old] Piepenbrock et al. (as summarized by NN/g): dark text on light background beat light on dark on acuity and proofreading tasks for young (18-33) and older (60-85) adults; benefit was smaller for older adults; at night light mode led, no significant difference in simulated daytime; no polarity difference in fatigue; participants did not perceive a readability difference. Dark mode may help some low-vision users (e.g., cataracts). — [NN/g dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/) [Industry summary of peer-reviewed work]

**Dyslexia and low vision**
- [Rep, children with dyslexia n=170 + 147] Kuster et al. (2018, Annals of Dyslexia): the Dyslexie font gave no speed or accuracy benefit over Arial in text reading (n = 170) or word lists (102 dyslexic, 45 non-dyslexic children); most preferred Arial/Times, and preference did not relate to performance. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6)
- [Rep, adults with and without dyslexia] Sjoblom, Eaton & Stagg (2016, British Journal of Educational Psychology): large letter spacing improved reading speed in general and accuracy in dyslexic adults (24 dyslexic + 24 controls); coloured overlays did not help. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12127) Extra-large letter spacing in children (Zorzi et al. 2012) is the original positive result; a partial replication (Duranovic et al. 2018) found accuracy but not speed gains; reports that Dyslexie's advantage over Arial vanished when Arial was given the same spacing (Marinus et al. 2016); Wery & Diliberto (2017) found no OpenDyslexic benefit. — [secondary web summary](https://www.readandspell.com/best-font-for-dyslexia) and [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700) [Snippet; primaries not opened]
- A 2023 single-case study of three Turkish 10-year-olds claims OpenDyslexic improved fluency and comprehension; n = 3, no control, low weight. — [Karatay & Ünal 2023](https://doi.org/10.29329/tayjournal.2023.537.11) [Single]
- [Rep, low vision] Letter case and size: uppercase read faster than lowercase at small sizes, similar at large sizes (Arditi & Cho 2007). — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)

### Inferences
- Most robust and practically useful: (a) avoid shrinking type below comfortable size, especially for older or low-vision readers; (b) keep lines near 50 to 75 characters (comprehension) unless speed is the priority; (c) default to normal letter spacing and common familiar typefaces; (d) dark-on-light in normal lighting; (e) for important, complex, informational text read under time pressure, paper or a large stable screen layout shows a small but real advantage.
- The screen-inferiority effect is small (about 0.1 to 0.25 SD), appears to have declined for handheld devices, and may partly reflect shallower reading and overconfidence (calibration g = 0.20) rather than legibility itself. It is a population-average effect, so it will rarely be visible for an individual reader.
- Preference is not performance (Wallace 2022; Kuster 2018; Piepenbrock): asking readers what they like is a poor guide to which layout is faster.

### Gaps
- Legge & Bigelow (2011) critical print size review and Chung/Legge line-spacing work could not be retrieved (403), so critical print size and fluent range numbers are not stated. No controlled evidence on full justification vs ragged right on screen was retrieved. Delgado et al.'s subgroup effect sizes (time-constrained vs self-paced) were not available from the primary text (one secondary report cites g = -0.26 for time-constrained, unverified). No post-2023 meta-analysis of the screen effect was found, and Furenes et al. (2021) / Kong et al. (2018) were not retrieved. Line-spacing optimum has no clear quantitative figure.

## Disfluency: do harder-to-read fonts improve learning?

### Takeaway
No. The original positive findings (Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer & Vaughan 2011) did not hold up: a meta-analysis found zero average effect, multiple replications failed, and Sans Forgetica produced no memory advantage. Disfluent fonts mainly act as a metacognitive cue (readers judge they learned less, and study longer).

### Cited Findings
- [Single, original] Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer & Vaughan (2011, Cognition): Study 1 (lab) reported harder-to-read fonts increased retention; Study 2 extended it to real classrooms. Secondary source puts Study 1 at 28 adults and Study 2 at 222 high school students (unverified). — [abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=Fortune+favors+the+bold+and+the+italicized+disfluency); [HSE working paper](https://wp.hse.ru/data/2021/11/10/1458461373/127PSY2021.pdf) [Snippet]
- [MA] Xie, Zhou & Liu (2018, Educational Psychology Review): 25 articles, 3,135 participants: effect of perceptual disfluency on recall d = -0.01, on transfer d = 0.03; no moderator (participant, material, design) mattered. Disfluency lowered judgments of learning and increased learning time. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x); [PubMed-linked summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7854329/)
- [Reproducibility critique] Weißgerber, Brunmair & Rummer (2021): found errors in coding and effect sizes in Xie et al.; the transfer result is "rather robust" but recall and moderator analyses are doubtful. They criticize transparency, not the null conclusion. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1)
- [Rep, failed replications] Rummer, Schweppe & Schwede (2016, Metacognition and Learning): null effects of disfluency on learning outcomes, including a replication of the original alien-description paradigm; Eitel et al. (2014): four multimedia experiments, only the first showed a benefit; Geller et al. (2018) among negative results; a 2023 pre-registered direct replication (N = 96) of a disfluency-by-working-memory study was null for all outcomes. — [Rummer et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-015-9151-5); [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.678798/epub); [2023 replication](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11409-023-09366-7) [Snippet]
- [Rep, Sans Forgetica] Taylor et al. (2020, Memory): four experiments; Sans Forgetica rated harder to read but yielded equivalent, and sometimes lower, memory than Arial; word pairs: fewer remembered in Sans Forgetica; passages: 74% vs 73% accuracy. The font creators' claim (unpublished; reportedly 400 students, 57% vs 50% recall) was never published. — [paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/hard-read-font-was-designed-boost-memory-it-might-not-actually-work); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/previously-claimed-memory-boosting-font-sans-forgetica-does-not-actually-boost-memory)
- A separate preliminary result: one study reported no benefit under low test expectancy but an improvement under high test expectancy. — [BPS digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/hard-read-font-was-designed-boost-memory-it-might-not-actually-work) [Snippet]
- Ho Sang & Petrarca (2025) still list one study in which disfluent conditions yielded higher test scores, showing the positive claim persists in the literature despite null aggregates. — [Visible Language PDF](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)

### Inferences
- Treat deliberately degraded fonts as unsupported for learning; if anything they slow reading and add load. The "desirable difficulty" in fonts is not a robust effect; the confirmed effect is on confidence and study time.

### Gaps
- Original Diemand-Yauman effect sizes and sample sizes not verified from the primary paper. Weißgerber's corrected numeric estimates not extracted.

## How people read on screens: scanning, F-pattern and the NN/g findings

### Takeaway
NN/g's claims (users scan, F-pattern, "concise, scannable, objective" gives +124% usability) are plausible and directionally consistent with other work, but the primary evidence is small, partly confounded, industry-reported, and the F-pattern has no published statistics and no peer-reviewed replication. Treat the specific numbers as illustrations, not estimates.

### Cited Findings
- [Industry, 1997] Morkes & Nielsen: three studies, 81 participants in total (11 directed tasks + 19 think-aloud + 51 measurement experiment). Study 3: between-subjects, 5 conditions (promotional control, concise, scannable, objective, combined) on a seven-page fictional "Travel Nebraska" site; means (control vs combined): task time 359 s vs 149 s, task errors 0.82 vs 0.10, memory 0.41 vs 0.67, sitemap time 185 vs 130 s, satisfaction 5.7 vs 7.0. Composite usability (control = 100): combined 224, concise 158, scannable 147, objective 127; the "124% improvement" is the 224 composite. Conditions per cell roughly 10 (inference from df). — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)
- [Industry, reliability caveats] Statistical tests were one-tailed, no multiple-comparison correction, several results marginal (p<.10), one site, manipulations confounded (several features varied at once), 1997 participants excluding web professionals; the composite is highly sensitive to the tiny control error mean (recalculation without the error measure gave about 161 rather than 224, per the fetching tool's analysis). — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/) (the authors themselves write "More research is obviously needed")
- [Industry] Scanning: 15 of 19 think-aloud participants scanned before reading (this matches the oft-quoted "79% always scanned, 16% read word by word"); 10 of 19 disliked hard-to-read writing. — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/); [Alertbox 1997 mirror](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web)
- [Single, observational, academic] Weinreich et al. (2008, ACM Transactions on the Web), data from 2005: 25 users, 45,237 analysed page views (after dropping 24% of 59,573); visit time about 25 s + 4.4 s per 100 words; users could read at most about 28% of the words on an average 593-word page, realistically about 20%; about 18% more text readable per extra 100 words. NN/g published this as "How Little Do Users Read?". Caveats: time on page is not reading time, assumed 250 wpm, above-average-education sample, judgement-based filtering, and the fetching tool flagged that 28% does not follow cleanly from the stated formula. — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)
- [Industry] F-pattern (Nielsen 2006): 232 users, "thousands of pages", only three heatmaps shown (an About-us article, an e-commerce product page, a search results page); no statistics (no percentages, tests or intervals); no description of recruitment, equipment, page or task selection. The author calls the F a "rough, general shape", with E and inverted-L variants and layout dependence. Claims: users read the left edge of lines and subheads closely; start headings, paragraphs and bullets with information-carrying words; the first two paragraphs carry the key points. A later NN/g follow-up eye-tracking study is linked but not summarized. — [NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)
- [Industry vendor, contrary] EyeQuant (2017): 99 websites x 46 participants with realistic tasks, first 15 seconds on average: none of the individual heat maps closely resembled an F; the bias was toward the centre-left of the screen. Vendor with commercial stake in eye tracking. — [EyeQuant](https://eyequant.com/resources/eye-tracking-studies-does-the-famous-f-shape-pattern-really-exist)
- [Preprint, 2026] A reanalysis of an eye-tracking dataset found no consistent F-shape in carousel interfaces: dual-focus on the first page, mirrored F-shapes after swiping. — [arXiv 2604.21019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21019)
- Early informal critiques (2006 SIGIA-L list): F-pattern may be generalisation outside experimental conditions or an artifact of normal reading and paragraph gaps. — [SIGIA-L archive](https://asist-archive.ischool.illinois.edu/sigia-l/2006-April/017218.html) [informal]

### Inferences
- The robust, replicated part of this literature is that web users sample rather than read exhaustively and that time on page scales weakly with page length; the shape of that sampling is task- and layout-dependent, so the F-pattern should be seen as a common but not universal tendency rather than a law.
- The +124% should not be quoted as an effect size: it is a composite of five measures from a small, confounded, one-tailed experiment on a single simple site. The directional finding (shorter, scannable, objective text improved task speed and satisfaction) matches the headings/search evidence from the Lorch programme.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed replication of Morkes & Nielsen's composite was found; no peer-reviewed validation of the F-pattern frequency; the full Weinreich et al. paper was not read (only NN/g's account). NN/g's later eye-tracking confirmation study was not summarized. The "F-pattern vs task type" literature (e.g., task-dependent scanning) was not retrieved.
