# Modality, effort measurement and hard limits on language intake (healthy skilled adults, own language)

Scope: how comprehension effort is measured; how channel (reading, listening, audiovisual, speeded presentation) changes comprehension, speed and effort; hard limits on intake rate. Word choice, syntax, telegraphic style, typography and layout are out of scope (other researchers). Research window: up to November 2026.

Evidence tags used in every bullet:
- **[S]** strong: meta-analysis or large, replicated, multi-lab result.
- **[M]** moderate: several converging studies, a single large study, or a well-established review with soft quantification.
- **[W]** weak: single small study, abstract-only reading, press or aggregator summary, theory without direct test.
- **[D]** disputed or unreplicated.

Reading depth (so the report-writer can judge reliability): I read full text of Brysbaert 2019, Coupé et al. 2019 (via PMC), Clinton-Lisell 2023 (reading-while-listening), Zheng and Meister (arXiv), the Arons time-compressed-speech review, and (via a reader mirror, first ~100k characters) Rayner et al. 2016. Everything else was read at abstract level or through search-engine summaries of abstracts; those are marked "abstract-level" and numbers should be re-checked against the primary before quoting. Where a cited URL is an aggregator, the bibliographic details were taken from the aggregator and the DOI should be preferred for the final report.

## 1. How is language-comprehension effort measured, and do the measures agree?

### Takeaway
Effort has no gold-standard measure. Eye movements index moment-to-moment processing difficulty (largely driven by word predictability), pupil size indexes autonomic arousal and resource allocation, EEG markers (N400, alpha, theta) index different sub-processes, fMRI shows a dedicated language network that responds to surprisal while the domain-general multiple-demand network responds mostly to task demands, and self-report scales measure perceived effort. These measures correlate weakly with one another (only about 36% of 274 reported correlations in listening-effort research were significant, mostly "poor or fair"), so "least taxing" depends on which dimension of effort is meant.

### Cited Findings

**Eye tracking (fixation duration, regressions, skipping)**
- Typical adult English reading: fixations about 250 ms (mean of 10 skilled readers 216 ms, range 190-255), forward saccades about 7 letters (Table mean 8.1 characters), regressions 10-15% of eye movements, next word skipped about 30% of the time ("the" about 50%), perceptual span about 3-4 letters left and 14-15 letters right of fixation (Chinese: 1 character left, 3 right); eye movements themselves occupy at most about 10% of reading time. [M] — [Rayner et al. 2016, PSPI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267) (full text via reader mirror, first 100k characters; the "10%" is from the abstract-level press summary at [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/01/160114163035.htm)).
- Rayner's 1998 review frames eye-movement data as reflecting "moment-to-moment cognitive processes" (perceptual span, integration across saccades, individual differences incl. dyslexia). [M] (abstract-level) — [Rayner 1998, Psychological Bulletin 124:372-422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9849112/)
- Word predictability affects reading time logarithmically: six reading datasets plus several language models, nonlinear regression, favours a logarithmic (surprisal) rather than linear effect. [M-S] (abstract-level; replicated across datasets) — [Shain, Meister, Pimentel, Cotterell & Levy 2024, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576)
- Reading for meaning versus scanning for a word is visible in eye movements: scanning has longer forward saccades, shorter fixations, and fixation durations much less affected by word frequency; classifiers on eye-movement data separate reading-for-meaning from search with 60-85% accuracy. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019, JML 109:104047](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf) (full text; cites Rayner & Fischer 1996, Simola et al. 2008, Biedert et al. 2012)
- Regressions are a strategy marker, not only an effort marker: when studying a text, slower pace (about 130 wpm) came from regressions to topic heads and summaries, not from longer fixations; those "topic-structure" readers had the best summaries (83% correct) vs slow linear readers (66%). [M] — Hyönä, Lorch & Kaakinen 2002 as reported in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Mind wandering during reading shows in eye movements: more skipped words and fewer fixations (meta-analysis of 16 datasets from 19 articles). [M] (abstract-level) — [Mezière et al. 2025, Memory & Cognition](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/504749065?lang=en_US)
- In sped-up video watching (0.75x-2x, 38 undergraduates), mean fixation duration fell at 1.75x and 2x (about -26 to -34 ms), but pupil diameter showed no speed effect; recall of low-working-memory participants fell from 1.25x, high-working-memory participants showed no recall drop. [W] (single small study, 2026) — [Chung, Nam & Hong 2026, Cyberpsychology 20(4)](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/42432)

**Pupillometry**
- Pupil size is a composite of autonomic nervous system activity, not effort alone; the response is non-linear (small for very easy tasks and also small for very hard tasks where listeners disengage). [M] — [Winn, Wendt, Koelewijn & Kuchinsky 2018, Trends in Hearing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6166306) (abstract-level)
- Classic result: object-relative and filler-gap sentences took longer to read and gave larger pupil dilation than simpler sentences (pupil as "intensity" of processing). [M] (abstract-level) — [Just & Carpenter 1993, Can. J. Exp. Psychol. 47:310](https://scilit.com/publications/d39e8c9592cbe05eb27f1e2140e7d26a)
- Adding a memory load can cancel the usual link between speech clarity and pupil dilation, so pupil results depend on concurrent demands. [W] (search-summary level; source not individually identified; see Gaps)

**EEG (N400, P600, alpha, theta)**
- N400: amplitude varies with linguistic and non-linguistic manipulations of meaning-related processing while latency is nearly constant; elicited by words, pseudowords, pictures, sounds. [S as a descriptive review] (abstract-level) — [Kutas & Federmeier 2011, Annu. Rev. Psychol. 62:621](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/thirty-years-and-counting-finding-meaning-in-the-n400-component-of-the-event-related-brain-potential-erp)
- N400 and prediction: a 9-lab, N=334 pre-registered replication reproduced the noun-predictability N400 effect but not the article effect (the evidence that readers routinely pre-activate the phonological form of upcoming words); a non-zero article effect, if any, is far smaller than first reported. [S for the failed replication] — [Nieuwland et al. 2018, eLife](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/130281/1/elife_33468_v2.pdf)
- Alpha power (about 10 Hz): rose with both memory load and acoustic degradation, with super-additive combination (Obleser et al. 2012); in an irrelevant-speech paradigm alpha tracked top-down control set by listening goals rather than acoustics alone (Wöstmann, Lim & Obleser 2017). [M] (abstract-level) — [Wöstmann et al. 2017 record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/the-human-neural-alpha-response-to-speech-is-a-proxy-of-attention/); Obleser 2012 and Petersen 2015 summarised in [Petersen et al. 2015, Front. Psychol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00177/full)
- Frontal-midline theta increases with working-memory load in non-language tasks (Gevins 1997; Onton, Delorme & Makeig 2005) and with antecedent retrieval across a clause boundary in sentence comprehension (Gaston et al. 2015, Cortex). [W-M] (abstract-level) — [Gaston et al. record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/56d8f918dabfae2eeea7027d/frontal-posterior-theta-oscillations-reflect-memory-retrieval-during-sentence-comprehension); [Onton talk summary](https://sccn.ucsd.edu/~julie/SPR2005Talk-Theta.html)
- I found no study that measures P600 and frontal-midline theta in the same sentence paradigm, and no review linking them. [gap, see below]

**fMRI: language network vs multiple-demand (MD) network (Fedorenko lab)**
- Pooled 30 word/sentence comprehension experiments (481 participants, 678 scanning sessions): MD regions respond during comprehension only when an explicit task is present, not during passive reading/listening (which strongly drives the language network); MD responds more to lists of unconnected words than to sentences. Authors conclude MD engagement reflects effort tied to extraneous task demands, not core sentence processing (not inhibition of irrelevant meanings, not holding intermediate representations, not prediction). [M-S] — [Diachek, Blank, Siegelman, Affourtit & Fedorenko 2020, J Neurosci 40(23)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/133763) (abstract-level)
- The language network's response is best predicted by sentence surprisal and well-formedness: an encoding model trained on fMRI responses to 1,000 sentences selected new sentences that raised or lowered activity in new participants (preprint figures: about +86% and about -98% relative to diverse natural sentences). [M] — [Tuckute et al. 2024, Nat Hum Behav 8:544](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/153265); percentages from the [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.04.16.537080v4.full.pdf+html) and may differ in the final paper.
- Semantic tuning maps across cortex during listening vs reading of the same stories agree strongly; a model fit on one modality predicts the other. So the semantic code, once reached, is amodal. [M] — [Deniz, Nunez-Elizalde, Huth & Gallant 2019, J Neurosci](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6764208) (abstract-level)

**Self-report (Paas scale, NASA-TLX)**
- Paas's 9-point symmetrical mental-effort scale is a single-item, unidimensional scale; an instrument database lists test-retest .90 and cites it as sensitive to small task-complexity changes; critics (Ayres 2018) question reliability and validity of subjective load scales. [W-M] — [Learning Lab instrument entry](https://learninglab.uni-due.de/research-instrument/13339); [Ouwehand et al. 2021, Front. Educ.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.702616) (numerical formats tracked complex problem solving better than pictorial formats)
- NASA-TLX: test-retest ICC .71-.81 in older adults (good to excellent); a 2025 HCI review and validation found weak convergent validity and sensitivity for subjective workload scales and urges caution; one critique says it may measure perceived difficulty rather than workload; it is retrospective so gives no continuous data. [M-W] — [Older-adult psychometrics](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4ORJ0VN7); [TU Delft "Should we use the NASA-TLX in HCI?"](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:c784a7b2-7837-47ee-af3d-6d06e3c5be2d)
- In a multimedia-learning comparison, NASA-TLX and Paas ratings were both sensitive to intrinsic and extraneous load; weighted TLX added little over unweighted. [W] (record metadata inconsistent; verify date) — [Datalearner record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/118/paper-detail/72324)

**Dual-task methods**
- Review of 29 dual-task studies of listening effort in younger and older adults: dual-task paradigms detected effort differences between conditions, but task designs varied widely and there is no standard procedure. [M] — [Gagné, Besser & Lemke 2017, Trends in Hearing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5308443)
- A conference abstract reports prioritisation instructions did not reliably keep participants on the primary task, which would undermine interpretation. [W] (not confirmed peer-reviewed) — [Kestens et al., SFORL abstract](https://mediatheque.sforl.org/wp-content/uploads/202_Katrien_KESTENS.pdf)

**Agreement between measures**
- 116 adults (normal to severe hearing loss), simultaneous pupil size, EEG alpha, skin conductance and self-rated effort: pairwise correlations weak or non-significant, even though test-retest reliability was mostly good (ICC ≥ .71 except skin conductance and the fatigue scale), so low reliability does not explain low agreement. Factor analysis gave four dimensions; pupillometry formed its own cluster, self-rated effort grouped with baseline alpha. Authors: "different measures of listening effort should not be used interchangeably." [M-S] — [Alhanbali, Dawes, Millman & Munro 2019, Ear & Hearing 40:1084](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7664710/) (note: some secondary sources misdate this to 2017)
- Systematic review of 48 papers, 274 correlational analyses: 99 (36.1%) significant; most significant ones rated poor or fair; strong ones depended on experimental conditions; evidence quality graded "very low"; authors suggest measuring consequences of effort rather than effort itself. [S] (systematic review, abstract-level) — [Shields et al. 2023, Trends in Hearing 27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9982391)
- Modality caveat: nearly all of the multi-measure validation work is in hearing science (degraded speech, older or hearing-impaired participants), not in reading; the blink effect even reverses direction between a reading literature (blinks suppressed as load rises) and one sentence-listening study (blinks decrease with harder listening), so measures may not transfer across modality. [W] (search summary of a 2025 preprint listing; not individually verified)

### Inferences
- "Effort" is at least three constructs: (a) per-word processing difficulty (eye movements, N400, language-network fMRI; driven by surprisal), (b) resource allocation or arousal (pupil, alpha), (c) subjective, metacognitive effort (Paas, TLX). They dissociate empirically (Alhanbali; Shields), so any claim that one way of taking in language is "least taxing" must name which construct, and total effort, effort per unit time, and effort per unit of information retained can rank channels differently.
- The Fedorenko result implies that passive, comprehension-only intake does not recruit the generic effort network; much of the "effort" seen in studies with comprehension questions or odd tasks is task-induced. For choosing a low-effort reading style, the language-network response to surprisal is the relevant "core" cost, and the MD network cost is largely about what the reader is asked to do with the text.
- No study I found compares reading and listening of the same text with pupillometry, EEG alpha/theta, or dual-task methods head to head in skilled adults. The strongest physiological statement available is indirect: semantic representation is modality-invariant (Deniz 2019), and behavioural rates converge (see sections 2 and 3).

### Gaps
- No direct physiological effort comparison of reading vs listening vs video for the same material in healthy adults (the search I ran for it returned only listening-effort studies).
- One search snippet mentioned a critique that pupil patterns seen during whole-sentence reading may reflect gaze-related measurement error; I could not identify the source, so it is not cited.
- Kutas & Federmeier 2011, Rayner 1998, Winn 2018 and Shields 2023 were read only at abstract level; specific effect sizes of N400/fixation measures against difficulty were not extracted.
- Frontal theta evidence is indirect (memory-load and retrieval studies), and I found no consolidated review for language effort.
- The Paas test-retest value (.90) comes from an instrument database entry without sample details; the original Paas 1992/1994 papers were not retrieved.

## 2. Reading versus listening versus audiovisual: what do meta-analyses show?

### Takeaway
For skilled adults the overall comprehension difference between reading and listening is small and not reliable (g = 0.07, 46 studies). The channel advantage depends on conditions: reading wins modestly when it is self-paced, and on inference questions (g about 0.36); listening plus text helps mainly when reading is experimenter-paced (g = 0.41) and adds nothing when reading is self-paced (g = 0.06). Video's benefit is mainly relative to conventional teaching, not relative to text, and the "modality principle" in multimedia learning is a different comparison (narration vs on-screen text beside diagrams).

### Cited Findings
- **Reading vs listening, meta-analysis**: 46 studies, N = 4,687, robust variance estimation. Overall g = 0.07 (p = .23, no reliable difference). Self-paced reading advantage g = 0.13 (p = .049); experimenter-paced g = -0.32 (p = .16, not reliable). Inferential comprehension g = 0.36 (p = .02); general comprehension g = 0.15 (p = .05); literal comprehension g = -0.01 (p = .93). Reading and listening looked more similar in transparent-orthography languages (tentative). [S for the null overall; M for moderators] (abstract-level via aggregator) — [Clinton-Lisell, Review of Educational Research, DOI 10.3102/00346543211060871](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1476650676912693248) (online Dec 2021, listed as 2022 in print; see also [UND repository](https://commons.und.edu/ehb-fac/75))
- **Reading while listening vs reading only**: 30 studies, 62 effect sizes, N = 1,945. Overall g = 0.18 (SE 0.07, 95% CI 0.04-0.32; "trivial"), I² = 99.8%. Experimenter-paced reading comparisons: g = 0.41 (9 studies, 15 ES, CI 0.13-0.70); self-paced: g = 0.06 (24 studies, 47 ES, CI -0.07-0.19, p = .34). Possible small-study publication bias in the funnel plot, Egger's test not significant (p = .12). Struggling readers and L2 incidental vocabulary may benefit, too few studies to generalise. [M-S] (full text) — [Clinton-Lisell 2023, Educ. Res. Theory & Practice 34(3)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)
- **Audiobook vs e-text vs both (91 adults, nonfiction)**: no significant differences in comprehension immediately or after two weeks; e-text was on screen, not paper. [W-M] (abstract-level; single study) — [Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal 2016, SAGE Open 6(3)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v6y2016i3p2158244016669550.html) (one newsletter mis-cites it as J Educ Psychol)
- **Screens vs paper** (text medium, reading comprehension): meta-analysis k = 56, g = -0.21 (95% CI -0.28 to -0.14) against screens; only reliable under time constraints (g about -0.26) and for informational (expository) rather than narrative texts; effect grew about 0.01 per year 2001-2017; no grade-level effect. [S] (abstract-level via secondary sources; author list varies between records) — [Delgado et al. 2018, Educational Research Review 25:23-38](https://producciocientifica.uv.es/documentos/5ee0c83b2999527595f98a60)
- **Video vs conventional teaching**: 105 randomised trials, about 7,776 students. Replacing conventional teaching with video g = 0.28; adding video to existing teaching g = 0.80; video more effective for skills than knowledge. This is video versus lecture/classroom, not video versus text. [M-S] (abstract-level) — [Noetel et al. 2021, Review of Educational Research 91:204-236](https://www.aera.net/tabid/19324/mid/50329/default.aspx); a 2024 preprint disputes the skills/knowledge split.
- **Modality principle (narration vs on-screen text beside pictures)**: re-analysis of 91 studies found narration advantages d = 0.24 (retention) and d = 0.25 (transfer); larger for system-paced presentation (d = 0.43/0.44), animations (0.50/0.59) and short materials (0.38/0.33); no reverse effect found. Mayer's chapter reports a median d = 1.02 across 17 transfer tests (not a meta-analysis; selected tests). One study found the pattern reversed after a one-day delay (visual-only learners higher). [M-W] (search-level; Ginns 2005 original not retrieved) — [Wang, Xie & Li 2016 (Adv. Psychol. Sci.)](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/adps/EN/Y2016/V24/I3/335); [Mayer, Modality principle chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/modality-principle/E5CD6E01CEA0B568CE260F66A3CD0D1F)
- **Mind wandering by channel**: reading aloud gave least mind wandering, listening to a read passage gave most, with poorest memory and least interest for the listening condition (within-subject). [W-M] — [Varao Sousa, Carriere & Smilek 2013, Front. Psychol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00892/pdf). Live vs recorded lecture: memory better after live lecture, overall mind-wandering rate did not differ, interest and motivation higher for live. [W-M] — [Varao-Sousa & Kingstone 2015, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4641615)
- **Brain-level equivalence**: see Deniz et al. 2019 (section 1): semantic selectivity maps for listening and reading of the same narratives are interchangeable. [M]
- **Why channel differences mostly vanish**: Brysbaert argues silent reading at 238-260 wpm sits at the maximum rate at which compressed speech is still understood (about 260 wpm), and that groups who cannot read that fast (children, older adults, L2 readers) also cannot process 260 wpm speech; so no reading-specific language process needs to be postulated. [M] (hypothesis from full-text meta-analysis, indirect) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- **When one channel wins (summary of the above)**: self-paced reading beats experimenter-paced listening (g = 0.13); inference questions favour reading (g = 0.36); text difficulty is confounded and not cleanly estimated (no reliable difficulty moderator reported in the abstract-level sources); reading-while-listening helps only when plain reading is time-limited or experimenter-paced (g = 0.41 vs 0.06); screens lose to paper under time pressure on expository text (g about -0.26).

### Inferences
- The consistent moderator is **who controls the pace** (and with it the ability to regress or re-read), not the sensory channel. Listening, by default, is system-paced and non-revisitable, which probably explains the small reading advantage on inferential questions (inference needs re-integration), and why adding audio to self-paced reading is redundant.
- A channel comparison at equal speeds is ill-defined: self-paced reading averages 238-260 wpm while audiobooks are 140-180 wpm (Brysbaert), so equal-time comparisons favour reading, whereas equal-text comparisons need listening at about 1.5x. Arithmetic: 150 wpm x 1.5 = 225 wpm, close to typical silent reading speed; 2x gives about 300 wpm, above the 275 wpm knee of the speech-compression literature (section 3).
- For "least taxing" the evidence supports no universal channel winner for skilled adults on ordinary prose; it supports giving the person control of pacing and the ability to repeat.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis directly comparing reading vs listening vs video for the same expository material with effort measures.
- Text-difficulty moderator for reading vs listening not extracted (full text of Clinton-Lisell RER not accessible; the SAGE page returned 403).
- Captioned/subtitled video and dual-coded (audio plus text) comprehension meta-analyses (e.g. Montero Perez et al.) were not searched.
- Ginns 2005 modality meta-analysis effect sizes not retrieved from the original.
- Whether Rogowsky 2016 and the pacing finding replicate with modern TTS voices: unaddressed.

## 3. Typical and maximal rates; what breaks first

### Takeaway
Skilled adults read silently at about 238 wpm (non-fiction) to 260 wpm (fiction), with a normal range of 175-300/200-320, speak at 130-200 wpm (audiobooks 140-180), and understand compressed speech well up to roughly 260-275 wpm, after which comprehension degrades faster. Lecture video at 1.5x costs little; at 2x and above the cost is moderate to large in the 2025 meta-analysis but small in an earlier experiment (disputed at 2x). Speech compression fails because of intelligibility loss of non-redundant words, loss of pauses, and short-term-memory overload, and it is partly trainable.

### Cited Findings
**Reading rates**
- 190 studies, 18,573 participants: English silent reading 238 wpm (non-fiction), 260 wpm (fiction); typical ranges 175-300 and 200-320 wpm; oral reading 183 wpm (77 studies, 5,965 participants). Valid for adults 18-60 without reading problems, native speakers, task-focused. The non-fiction/fiction gap is explained by word length: expected rate = 238 x 4.6 / mean word length. [S] — [Brysbaert 2019, JML 109:104047](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf) (full text)
- The often-cited 300 wpm benchmark traces to early work (Buswell treated it as a minimum) and, in Rayner's case, to a table of 10 MIT undergraduates (308 wpm) while larger contemporaneous samples read 190-265 wpm; manual timing of reading charts may inflate rates by about 10%. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Reading rate depends on purpose: for recall or studying, rates fall to about 100-150 wpm in many studies and as low as 54 wpm (Dirix et al. 2019: 54 wpm studying vs 189 wpm reading matched texts); Greene 1931: 212 wpm fast vs 104 wpm careful with notes; Kemper & Sumner 2001: 93 wpm when told to answer without consulting the text. Slower study rate arises from regressions and rereading rather than longer fixations; Brysbaert finds no evidence for separate "reading gears" except reading vs scanning. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Test reliability: reading rate reliability about .7 for the Nelson-Denny test, r = .95 for two 7,300-word texts, r = .86 across three stories; at least 5 minutes of reading is needed for a stable estimate. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)

**Speaking and listening rates**
- Normal English speaking is 130-200 wpm; talkers can compress their own speech only to about 70% of normal duration; text-to-speech synthesisers reach about 550 wpm; the Guinness record speaker was clocked at 586 wpm. [M] (review) — [Arons, time-compressed speech review, MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mas-632-conversational-computer-systems-fall-2008/ee5c83dac8079f6d25bb657f5ed393bd_arons_timecomp.pdf)
- Comfortable speech about 150-160 wpm; doubling an audiobook reaches only about 300 wpm. [M] — [Rayner et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267)
- Ideal radio-news speech rate 170 wpm (high-density content) to 190 wpm (low-density) in Rodero's work; Jester & Travers 1966 found optimal 300 wpm for silent reading but 200 wpm for listening. [W-M] — as reported in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Read speech across 17 languages: mean 6.63 syllables/s (SD 1.15); individual averages 4.3-9.1 syllables/s. [S for read speech] — [Coupé et al. 2019, Sci. Adv.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6984970) (full text)

**Time-compressed speech (comprehension and what breaks first)**
- Foulke & Sticht (1969) review: comprehension declines slowly up to about 275 wpm and more rapidly beyond; the decline was not attributable to intelligibility alone but related to short-term-memory processing overload; experiments in French found no significant decay until about 300 wpm. [M] (reported in a review; primary not read) — [Arons review](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mas-632-conversational-computer-systems-fall-2008/ee5c83dac8079f6d25bb657f5ed393bd_arons_timecomp.pdf)
- Counter-view (Heiman et al. 1986): 50% compression removes essentially all redundant information, beyond that non-redundant information is lost; the compression ratio, not the 275 wpm word rate, is the crucial parameter, because too little of the signal arrives in too little time for enough words to be perceived. [M] — [Arons review](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mas-632-conversational-computer-systems-fall-2008/ee5c83dac8079f6d25bb657f5ed393bd_arons_timecomp.pdf)
- Preferences: sighted college students given speech at 175 wpm chose on average 82% of the time (about 212 wpm); blind subjects preferred 64-75% and 236-275 wpm, i.e. they trade extra listening effort for rate. [M] — [Arons review](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mas-632-conversational-computer-systems-fall-2008/ee5c83dac8079f6d25bb657f5ed393bd_arons_timecomp.pdf)
- Trainability: naive listeners tolerate up to about 50% compression, 8-10 hours of training allows substantially more, adaptation can take minutes, after about 30 minutes at compressed rates listeners found normal rate uncomfortable and preferred rates shifted faster over six weeks (informal/early studies). [W-M] — [Arons review](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/mas-632-conversational-computer-systems-fall-2008/ee5c83dac8079f6d25bb657f5ed393bd_arons_timecomp.pdf)
- Removing pauses makes speech "natural, but many people find it exhausting to listen to because the speaker never pauses for breath"; pauses help the listener keep pace. [W-M] — Arons review (quoting Neuburg 1978; Reich 1980)
- Gap restoration: speech compressed 3x was poorly intelligible (<50% words correct); cutting it into 40 ms fragments and inserting silent gaps of roughly 20-120 ms restored much of the intelligibility (U-shaped curve), with the repacking rate in the theta band; interpreted as giving the decoder extra time. [M] (abstract-level; replicated in modelling, not fully independent) — [Ghitza & Greenberg 2009, Phonetica 66:113](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1159/000208934)
- Acoustic speech envelope peaks at 4.3-5.4 Hz (syllable rhythm) across nine languages (25+ hours of speech). [M] — [Ding et al. 2017, Neurosci. Biobehav. Rev. 81:181](https://read.qxmd.com/read/28212857/temporal-modulations-in-speech-and-music)
- King & Behnke 1989: formal-lecture comprehension decreased as soon as the signal was compressed, while memory for digit/letter series and short dialogues held up to 45% compression (about 1.8x). Native speakers can repeat simple sentences compressed to 320 wpm (Conrad 1989). [W-M] — as reported in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Blind and trained listeners: blind participants followed formant-synthesised speech at up to about 17-22 syllables/s while sighted listeners' comprehension declined from about 9-14 syllables/s (tested at 9-14 for sighted, 17-22 for blind); an fMRI study links ultra-fast comprehension in the blind to visual cortex recruitment; confound: blind participants were also experienced screen-reader users, and a later study trained sighted people for about six months to test whether vision is the limit. [W-M] (conference-paper level) — [Moos & Trouvain, ICPhS 2007](https://icphs2007.de/conference/Papers/1186/index.html); [Dietrich et al. 2013, fMRI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3847124/)

**Sped-up video and podcasts**
- 231 students watched lectures at 1x, 1.5x, 2x, 2.5x: minimal costs from 1x to 1.5x or 2x, performance declined beyond 2x; watching twice at 2x gave no advantage over once at 1x, but re-watching at 2x just before the test beat one viewing at 1x a week earlier; students believed slower was better; authors warn of difficult material. [M] — [Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn & Castel, Applied Cognitive Psychology 36(1):69-82](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf); coverage in [THE](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/no-major-harm-learning-double-speed-lecture-viewing)
- Meta-analysis of 24 lecture-video studies (110 effect sizes, robust variance estimation): cost very small up to 1.5x, moderate to large at 2x and above; about 17 percentage points lower test scores at 2.5x (press report); older adults (61-94) hurt more than young in one included study; moderator analyses exploratory. [M-S] (abstract and press level) — [Tharumalingam, Roberts, Fawcett & Risko 2025, Educational Psychology Review](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/increasing-video-lecture-playback-speed-can-impair-test-performance-a-meta-analysis/1129892227363897361-10463); [press coverage](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250703-video-lecture-faster-speeds-disadvantages)
- An earlier meta-analysis (Cheng, Pastore & Ritzhaupt 2022) is reported as finding a small significant effect at 1.4-1.5x and a small non-significant effect at 1.8-2.0x (direction of the effect not confirmed). [D at 2x vs Tharumalingam] — [Cheng et al. record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/07356331211043535)
- Working-memory moderation (single 2026 study, N = 38): speeds 0.75x-2x; low-WM participants' recall dropped from 1.25x onward (log-odds -0.48 at 1.25x to -0.75 at 2x); high-WM participants showed no recall drop but shorter fixations at 1.75-2x; speed explained little variance (marginal R² about .01-.13); immediate recall only. [W] — [Chung et al. 2026](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/42432)

**L2, age**
- L2 reading: rates below 100 wpm not uncommon; Japanese university students: 139 wpm (those answering >75% right) vs 61 wpm; their optimal listening rates matched their reading rates; Dutch-English bilinguals read 17% slower in L2 with eye-movement patterns resembling children's; understanding of compressed speech drops faster in L2 users even when proficient (Conrad 1989). [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Older adults: decline in silent reading rate appears after about 60-65, with longer fixations, more skipping, more regressions, and parallel difficulty with compressed speech. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)

### Inferences
- Combining Brysbaert and Arons: the maximum comfortable single-pass rate for skilled adults is about 260-275 wpm in either modality; this coincidence suggests a shared late bottleneck, and is the best-supported "hard-ish" intake limit for continuous prose with full comprehension. Rates above it trade comprehension or require redundancy, familiarity, or training.
- Mapping to playback speed: typical audiobook/lecture rate 150 wpm x 1.5 = 225 wpm (inside the safe zone), x 2 = 300 wpm (past the knee), x 2.5 = 375 wpm. This matches the video literature where cost is small to 1.5x and grows at 2x+ (Tharumalingam), Why Murphy's 2x result is milder is unresolved; candidate explanations (my speculation, untested here) are lecture difficulty, original speaking rate and immediate rather than delayed testing.
- What breaks first (convergent but indirect): (1) non-redundant, low-frequency or unexpected words become unintelligible, (2) pauses that serve integration vanish, (3) working memory overloads, especially for low-WM listeners and for L2/older listeners, (4) delayed rather than immediate recall suffers. Speed benefits are therefore least risky for familiar, redundant content.
- Because students believed slower was better but performed equally at 2x, self-reported effort at high speeds may mislead; conversely rate preference and comprehension at higher speeds improve with exposure (trainability).

### Gaps
- Primary Foulke & Sticht 1969 and Heiman 1986 not read; 275 wpm is therefore a soft onset reported by a secondary review, and Heiman argues it is of little significance.
- Tharumalingam 2025 abstract not read directly; the 17-point figure is from press coverage.
- No source found for Tauroza & Allison 1990 genre-specific rates (searches returned citation metadata only); British English speech rate in 1990 not verified.
- No data found on effort (as opposed to comprehension) at high playback speeds beyond Chung 2026.

## 4. Speed reading, skimming and RSVP (Spritz-style) apps

### Takeaway
Doubling or tripling typical reading speed (about 250 to 500-750 wpm) without losing comprehension is not supported; the trade-off between speed and accuracy is robust, and eye movements are not the bottleneck (language processing is). RSVP can present short sentences at up to about 12 words/s with surprising retention, but for realistic passages comprehension falls and removing the ability to regress is costly (accuracy about 75% with regressions vs about 50%, chance, without).

### Cited Findings
- Review conclusion: faster reading costs accuracy; doubling or tripling a typical rate of about 250 wpm would likely leave comprehension below normal-pace reading; skimming is two to four times faster with lower comprehension; the dependable way to read faster is practice and stronger language skill ("language skill is at the heart of reading speed"). [M-S] (review; abstract plus full text) — [Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman 2016, PSPI 17(1):4-34](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/so-much-to-read-so-little-time-how-do-we-read-and-can-speed-readi/); full text at [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267)
- Trailing-mask experiment (each word masked after the eyes pass it, preventing re-reading): comprehension-question accuracy about 75% when regressions were allowed vs about 50% (chance) when not; deficits for both simple and ambiguous sentences; authors argue this undermines apps that eliminate eye movements and regressions. [M] — [Schotter, Tran & Rayner 2014, Psychological Science 25:1218](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614531148); numbers from the Rayner 2016 full text
- RSVP rates and errors: words typically shown about 100 ms each (about 600 wpm); Masson's study used 10 words/s; at 10 words/s readers reported "dack" as "deck" 40% of the time and "duck" as "deck" 26%, showing context-driven misperception at speed. [M] — [Rayner et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267) (the text cut off before later RSVP comprehension results)
- Potter's laboratory reports that a 14-word sentence can be understood and remembered at 12 words/s (720 wpm) and that coherent strings are retained much better than lists; longer passages have not shown the same lack of comprehension decrement. Compare this with Brysbaert's reading-rate ceiling: the sentence-level effect is a short, one-shot conceptual memory effect, not a sustainable passage rate. [W-M] (course-material/secondary level) — [MIT OCW 9.65 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/9-65-cognitive-processes-spring-2004/b6a884fd1caa9abb037acdb8e63678dd_feb25h04.pdf)
- Speed-reading app study: the comprehension loss tracked reading speed itself, and conventional page reading outperformed both RSVP presentation conditions; authors challenge the apps' claim that RSVP causes no comprehension deficits. [W-M] (abstract-level) — [Acklin & Papesh, Am. J. Psychol. 130(2), DOI 10.5406/amerjpsyc.130.2.0183](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1213063292418985988)
- Spritz marketing claim (readers can learn to double speed to 400-450 wpm without losing comprehension) is a vendor claim, contradicted by the reviews above. [W as evidence, useful as the claim being tested] — [Quartz coverage](https://qz.com/202698/speed-reading-apps-are-great-for-speed-and-terrible-for-reading-study-finds)
- Extreme claims: one reader reportedly read a Harry Potter book in about 47 minutes (over 4,200 wpm), another claims up to 30,000 wpm; Rayner et al. use a "Reading Efficiency Index" (wpm x comprehension) to show that 5,000 wpm at 60% comprehension is not credible. Brysbaert makes the same point with arithmetic: a Dutch newspaper edition of about 40,000 words "read" in one hour is 667 wpm, in half an hour 1,333 wpm, and reading only a 196-word abstract of a 35,000-word article at 238 wpm "reads" 42,000 words per minute, i.e. ultrafast "reading" is selective reading. [W] (anecdotal; used by reviews as illustrations) — [Rayner et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267); [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Skimming: Carver's "skimming" gear (450 wpm) and scanning (650 wpm) are not supported as separate qualitative gears except that scanning for a word differs from reading; skimming may be alternation between skipping and normal reading, an untested possibility (if so, information is found only where reading occurs). [M-W] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Incentives can raise speed with little comprehension loss: McConkie et al. found participants read faster when rewarded without much comprehension loss, and fast readers do not differ much in comprehension from average ones in a selected sample (curvilinear relation between rate and comprehension). [M] — McConkie & Rayner as discussed in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)

### Inferences
- Reading faster than the reader's own comfortable rate on dense prose moves along a speed-accuracy curve; no trick found that shifts the curve itself. Gains from "speed-reading training" are most plausibly practice and skimming (selective reading), not a new reading mechanism.
- RSVP removes regressions, enforces a pace, and strips parafoveal preview; all three go against what the self-pacing literature identifies as protective (sections 1-2). RSVP may still help for low-vision readers or for single short messages, but that is outside the evidence gathered here.
- Skimming is a legitimate, lower-effort strategy for goal-directed information finding, but it is a different task (lower comprehension), so it cannot be counted as a "less taxing way to take in language" without noting what is lost.

### Gaps
- Benedetto et al. 2015 (Spritz and visual fatigue) was not found in the search results; no direct evidence on RSVP fatigue was retrieved.
- A search snippet described a small study (30 German-speaking undergraduates) reporting about +35 wpm after app-based training without comprehension decline; I could not identify the source and do not rely on it.
- RSVP full results after the point where the Rayner 2016 text was cut off were not read.

## 5. Cross-language information rate and writing systems

### Takeaway
Spoken languages differ widely in syllable rate (about 5-8 syllables/s) and in information per syllable (about 4.8-8.0 bits), but these trade off (r = -0.71), producing a mean information rate of about 39 bits/s in read speech (SD 5.1; CV 13% vs 17% for speech rate). For reading, wpm tracks how many words a language needs to convey the same message, and matched-text studies find similar sentence reading times across Chinese, English and Finnish, but underpowered; no language or script is established as measurably more efficient.

### Cited Findings
- 17 languages from 9 families, 170 adult speakers, 15 parallel texts (about 240,000 syllables), read aloud: information density 4.8 bits/syllable (Basque) to 8.0 (Vietnamese); mean speech rate 6.63 syllables/s; mean information rate 39.15 bits/s (SD 5.10); speech rate vs density Pearson r = -0.71 (Spearman -0.70); Japanese and Spanish speakers produce about 50% more syllables/s than Vietnamese and Thai. [M-S] (full text) — [Coupé, Oh, Dediu & Pellegrino 2019, Science Advances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6984970); [CNRS summary](https://www.cnrs.fr/en/similar-information-rates-across-languages-despite-divergent-speech-rates)
- Caveats from the paper's own text: read, controlled speech (may not extend to casual or highly contextual conversation); canonical rather than realised syllables; information density is second-order (bigram, within-word) syllable entropy from written corpora, ignoring cross-word context and semantics, so it overestimates true information; correlational, trade-off not causally tested; unimodality tests gave conflicting results; per-language information rates are shown only in a figure. [M] — [Coupé et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6984970)
- Pellegrino, Coupé & Marsico 2011 (7 languages: British English, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Mandarin, Spanish): faster languages carry less information per syllable (Spanish fast and low-information; Mandarin slower and more informative). A press release says overall rates fell within about 4% of each other; the abstract states these strategies "do not necessarily produce a constant information rate". [M, with [D] on how constant "constant" is] (abstract-level) — [Pellegrino et al. 2011, Language 87:539](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/crosslanguage-perspective-on-speech-information-rate/73064B3F77E7CE81C42CAB29B4393F80); [press summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20110903/Study-sheds-new-light-on-the-ways-in-which-languages-ensure-efficient-communication-of-information.aspx)
- Reading rate by language (silent, wpm; number of studies): English 236 (144), Chinese 260 (18), German 260 (13), Italian 285 (3), Spanish 278 (6), Dutch 228 (7), Hebrew 224 (5), Korean 226 (5), Swedish 218 (5), French 214 (6), Finnish 195 (4), Arabic 181 (10); oral reading from 133 (Korean) to 191 (Spanish; English 190). Silent wpm correlates with a text expansion index (words needed relative to English): r = .55 (N = 12, directional test), oral r = .82, mean .75; silent vs oral rate across languages r = .50. Conclusion: information transferred, not word count, is what stays roughly constant. [M] (full text; small N of languages; Chinese words estimated at 1.5 characters per word, an overestimate of wpm if words are longer; Yan et al. 2010 report 1.87) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Matched-text comparisons: translated stories in English, Finnish and Mandarin gave no significant difference in time per sentence despite 1.5 characters/word (Chinese) vs 5.6 letters (English) and 1,301 (Finnish) vs 1,762 (English) words; Hebrew vs English Wikipedia paragraphs also no significant difference (56 per group). Brysbaert: both severely underpowered (a d = 0.4 difference needs at least 100 per language); Shimron & Sivan (1994) found slower Hebrew than English. [M-W] — Liversedge et al. 2016 and Kuperman et al. 2019 as reported in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- The Liversedge group's 2024 replication (about 80 new participants per language) found shorter total sentence reading times for Chinese readers, attributed to cultural change, while other findings held; they still argue for universals in reading. [D] (abstract-level) — [Liversedge et al. 2016, Cognition 147:1-20](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/2472368?lang=en_US) and [talk listing on the replication](https://www.city.ac.uk/news-and-events/events/2024/june/universality-in-eye-movements-and-reading-an-initial-study-and-a-replication-with-increased-power)
- Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus (13 languages): skipping rate varies considerably between languages, but average viewing time of fixated words is similar. [M] (abstract-level) — [Siegelman et al. 2022, Behav. Res. Methods 54:2843](https://www.utupub.fi/items/24876c19-2746-41f3-ba98-c35e41b73c53/full)
- Orthographic transparency: reading and listening comprehension looked more similar in transparent than opaque spelling systems (tentative). [W] — [Clinton-Lisell, RER](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1476650676912693248)
- Why wpm differs by language: function-word density (about 250 short function words), 44 vs 25 phonemes (Spanish), Latin alphabet vs Chinese characters, compounding conventions, and absence of word spaces in Chinese all alter average word length and thus wpm. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Speech information caveat in the "10 bits/s" literature: Zheng & Meister cite the 39 bits/s figure but note that it does not account for much redundancy in language, so the true rate is lower, though the same order of magnitude. [W-M, [D] on interpretation] — [Zheng & Meister, Neuron (Dec 2024) / arXiv 2408.10234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10234)

### Inferences
- The 39 bits/s figure is a property of produced speech per syllable-bigram entropy, not of comprehension or reading, so it should not be used as "human reading bandwidth". Rough reading estimate (my arithmetic, not a published figure): 238 wpm is about 4 words/s x about 5.6 characters including space = about 22 characters/s; at roughly 1 bit/character (the value Zheng & Meister use for English entropy) that is about 20-25 bits/s. This is below their fixation-based upper estimate of 28-45 bits/s because it accounts for skipping and the actual wpm data.
- Chinese at 260 wpm x 1.5 characters/word is about 390 characters/min (about 6.5/s), versus English roughly 1,300 characters/min (my arithmetic): characters carry more information than letters, so no inference about efficiency can be made from character counts, and wpm comparisons across languages are meaningless without translation-matched content.
- The weight of evidence points to approximately equal information throughput across languages and scripts in skilled readers; the residual differences (Chinese faster in the 2024 replication, Hebrew slower in earlier studies) are small, individually under-powered, and not yet settled.

### Gaps
- Per-language information rates (Japanese, Vietnamese, English) not retrievable from the PMC text, only from the figure.
- No definitive large-N cross-writing-system comparison of reading efficiency (the MECO corpus summarises eye-movement measures, not matched-information reading rates; I did not retrieve its reading rate values).
- Chinese "words" per minute depend on an estimated character-to-word conversion (1.5), so the 260 wpm value is approximate.
- Pellegrino et al. 2011: absolute bits/s values and the exact "within 4%" statistic were not verified against the paper.

## 6. Working memory, attention limits, and mind wandering during reading

### Takeaway
Working memory holds roughly 4 chunks (3-5), the language system must recode input quickly because raw sensory traces fade within roughly 50-100 ms (the now-or-never bottleneck, theoretically influential but not directly quantified), and overall conscious behavioural throughput has been estimated near 10 bits/s (a provocative, contested claim). Mind wandering during reading is common (36-42% of sentences in one probe study), more frequent and more damaging with difficult text, with pooled meta-analytic correlation with comprehension of only about -0.21.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan 2001: the "magical number 7" should be replaced by a central capacity limit of about 4 chunks (earlier estimates 3-5), operating alongside non-capacity-limited sources; capacity limits show up when chunking and rehearsal are controlled. [M] (abstract-level; physiological counting claims criticised in commentary) — [Cowan 2001, BBS 24:87-114](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/); critique in a [BBS commentary](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/which-brain-mechanism-cannot-count-beyond-four/6B69CAE24F4548E5CD9F714E902A4C91)
- Now-or-never bottleneck: because incoming language is transient, the brain must compress and recode it immediately into increasingly abstract chunks; acoustic traces are largely gone after about 50 ms and nearly absent after about 100 ms (press summary of cited work); the proposal generated 28 commentaries, including one arguing it is less tight than claimed. [W-M] (theory; abstract and press level) — [Christiansen & Chater 2016, BBS 39:e62](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/nowornever-bottleneck-a-fundamental-constraint-on-language/938D54E80A2A90A1C5990F4915B5E8D8); [ScienceDaily coverage](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/06/160610140815.htm)
- Brysbaert: reading and listening may share a bottleneck either because reading is phonologically recoded (verbal short-term memory relies on a phonological code) or because both must be converted to an abstract amodal memory code; the bottleneck would then be the rate at which abstract memory codes can be built and stored. Within-individual silent/oral relationship with zero intercept predicted by the compressed-speech view is untested. [W-M, theory] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Information-theoretic ceiling on behaviour: Zheng & Meister estimate human behavioural throughput at about 10 bits/s across typing (120 wpm x 5 chars x ~1 bit/char = 10 bits/s), speech (39 bits/s from Coupé, but lower after redundancy), listening comprehension of English (about 13 bits/s from 160 wpm narration), reading (28-45 bits/s from 200-250 ms fixations over 7-9 letters; speed-reading up to about 700 wpm or about 60 characters/s but with compromised comprehension), blindfolded speedcubing (11.8 bits/s), memory champions (about 5 bits/s). Critics note it omits unconscious signals; one reviewer found the argument "pretty airtight" for conscious tasks. [D / W-M; peer-reviewed in Neuron but a conceptual estimate] — [Zheng & Meister, arXiv 2408.10234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10234) (full text read); critique reported in [NYT summary by Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-speed-of-human-thought-lags-far-behind-your-internet-connection-study-finds/)
- Working memory and reading: meta-analysis of 197 studies (2,026 effect sizes) reports r = .29 (95% CI .27-.31) between working memory and reading (attribution of the exact figure to this paper is by inference from a search snippet); WM domain effects change with grade (verbal WM dominant from grade 4); the WM-comprehension link was partialled out when decoding and vocabulary were controlled. [S-M] (abstract-level) — [Peng et al. 2018, Psychological Bulletin 144:48-76](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/9832)
- Speech compression and WM: speech-compression comprehension failures were tied by Foulke & Sticht to short-term-memory overload, and the 2026 playback study found low-WM listeners lose recall earlier (see section 3). [W-M]
- Mind wandering and difficulty: with probes after sentences, readers reported off-task thoughts after 42% of sentences in difficult and 36% in easy texts; sentences read during mind wandering took 958 ms longer and comprehension questions were answered correctly 1.5 times less often; mind wandering was more frequent in difficult texts but more harmful to comprehension there. [M] — [Feng, D'Mello & Graesser 2013, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 20:586](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/17147); figures via [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf). A later dissertation argues the difficulty effect is confounded by differing section lengths in sentence-by-sentence presentation. [D] — [Forrin thesis, Waterloo](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/10738)
- Meta-analysis, mind wandering and reading comprehension: 25 full texts, 73 correlations, pooled r = -0.21; trait questionnaires (vs online probes) shift correlations by about 0.30 to null; moderators text type, length, difficulty, interest, working memory and language were not significant; stronger negative correlation with age. [S-M] (abstract-level) — [Bonifacci et al. 2022, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://www.pubmed.gov/35840838/)
- General mind-wandering/performance meta-analysis (k = 88): more off-task thought goes with worse performance, more so on more complex tasks (not longer tasks). [S] (abstract-level; pooled r not retrieved) — [Randall, Oswald & Beier 2014, Psychological Bulletin 140](https://www.pubmed.gov/25089941/)
- Channel and mind wandering: listening to a read passage produced the most mind wandering, reading aloud the least (Varao Sousa et al. 2013); live vs recorded lecture mind wandering did not differ overall but rose over time in recorded lectures per other work (see section 2). [W-M]
- Everyday reading is slower than lab reading because of mind wandering and interruptions. [W-M, authors' remark] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because working memory is small and the now-or-never constraint demands immediate chunking, anything that stretches the time between related pieces of information (very slow presentation) or compresses the time to chunk (very fast presentation) hurts. Brysbaert (citing Breznitz & Berman 2003; Kintsch & van Dijk 1978) notes each reader plausibly has an optimal input rate with comprehension falling both above it (overload) and below it (information arrives too slowly to integrate into chunks), i.e. an inverted-U. Whether slow presentation also invites mind wandering is plausible but I found no direct test, so treat it as a hypothesis.
- Mind wandering is a nuisance variable that differs by channel, text difficulty and interest (not cleanly separable in the literature), so effort and comprehension comparisons of channels should be measured with probes or eye-movement markers.
- The 10 bits/s figure is useful as an order-of-magnitude statement that conscious language intake of roughly 20-45 bits/s (reading) is not bandwidth-limited at the sensor but at later stages; it is not a precise limit.

### Gaps
- No direct meta-analysis of working-memory capacity vs speech-rate tolerance.
- Cowan 2001 and Christiansen & Chater 2016 read at abstract/press level; no quantification of "now-or-never" in bits/s or ms was found beyond the 50-100 ms trace-decay claim.
- Pooled r values for Randall et al. 2014 and Peng et al. 2018 not verified at primary level.
- Mind-wandering rates for audio versus text across matched difficulty, and with interest controlled, remain thinly tested.

## 7. Individual differences (skill, knowledge, WM, dyslexia, ADHD, L2) and the learning-styles hypothesis

### Takeaway
Reading rate is stable within persons (reliability up to .95 for long texts) and varies widely between persons (175-300 wpm for non-fiction), tracking spelling, vocabulary and crystallised intelligence more than working memory. L2 readers, older adults, children and struggling readers need slower rates; for struggling readers, text-to-speech has a small-to-moderate effect (d = .35). The learning-styles "matching" hypothesis (auditory learners should do better listening) has no support.

### Cited Findings
- Correlates of silent reading rate in 100 Dutch undergraduates (silent wpm / oral wpm): word spelling .51/.38, vocabulary .36/.10, crystallised IQ .35/.12, working memory .22/.19, fluid IQ .22/.08, conscientiousness .22/.11 (more conscientious students read slower), spoken text comprehension .19/.00; silent and oral rates correlated only r = .32 in that sample (.48 in two other studies). [M] (single sample, full text) — Callens, Tops & Brysbaert 2012 data as tabulated in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Rate and comprehension: little evidence that rate differences produce better or worse comprehension; fast readers are slightly better if anything; the relation is curvilinear (too slow and too fast both hurt) and confounded by reading difficulty, so correlations vary with text difficulty and sample. In Jackson & McClelland's small studies (the author flags low power), fast readers (>450 wpm) with comprehension equal to average readers (200-300 wpm) outperformed them on tasks involving meaningful stimuli (listening comprehension, letter-name matching, homophone decisions, category decisions) but not on visual discrimination or letter identification, and made more errors in the experimental tasks, suggesting a lower accuracy criterion. [W-M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Study-reading strategies cluster: about half of readers read linearly at 231 wpm, a quarter read linearly but slowly (133 wpm) and recalled worst (66%), and regressors at about 130 wpm recalled best (83%) when regressions targeted topic structure. [M] — Hyönä et al. 2002 in [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Children: silent reading rate increases through grade 12 (Taylor 1965, over 1,000 readers per grade; Spichtig et al. 2016 replication with 2,203 children found substantially lower rates); slower child reading shows longer and more fixations. [M] — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Struggling readers / reading disabilities: text-to-speech and read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension, random-effects mean d = .35 (95% CI .14-.56; 22 studies); mechanism: removes decoding demand. In the reading-while-listening meta-analysis, struggling readers may benefit but too few studies. [M-S] — [Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner 2018, J Learn Disabil 51:73](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/); [Clinton-Lisell 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)
- L2 readers: see section 3 (rates 61-139 wpm in the Hirai study, 17% slower in L2 for proficient bilinguals; listening limit tracks reading limit). [M]
- Working memory and reading: r = .29 meta-analytic (Peng et al. 2018, section 6). [S-M]
- Learning styles: Pashler et al. found "virtually no evidence" for the interaction pattern (matching instruction to preferred style improves learning) needed to justify learning-style assessments; few studies even used a design capable of testing it; stated preferences are not evidence of differential performance; not every model was tested. [S for the null as of 2008] (abstract-level) — [Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008, PSPI](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)
- Direct modality test: auditory vs visual preference did not predict comprehension from audiobook vs e-text, immediately or after two weeks (college-educated adults). [M] — [Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal 2015, J Educ Psychol 107:64-78](https://www.aminer.org/pub/56d92af5dabfae2eeed837bd/matching-learning-style-to-instructional-method-effects-on-comprehension); and the 2016 SAGE Open study (section 2). A fifth-grade follow-up (Frontiers in Psychology 2020) also found no support (non-adult sample).
- Prior knowledge: a thesis-based meta-analysis of 24 multiple-text comprehension studies found prior knowledge a significant contributor to comprehension (strong for semantic information and for integration across texts, moderate for evaluation and attending to sources); a separate meta-analysis found less benefit from prior knowledge for essays than stories. [W] (thesis and abstract level; not peer-reviewed in one case) — [Memphis thesis](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/etd/2119)

### Inferences
- The population-level "norm" rates (238 wpm, 260 wpm speech limit) are averages over large between-person differences (about ±60 wpm), so any recommended speed or channel for "least taxing" must be adjustable per person; the stable between-person spread, reliability near .86-.95, supports self-calibration.
- Verbal ability (vocabulary, spelling, crystallised knowledge) predicts rate more than working memory or fluid intelligence, consistent with Rayner et al.'s "language skill is at the heart of reading speed".
- For readers with decoding difficulties, the channel does matter: TTS gives a meaningful comprehension benefit (d = .35). For skilled adults, the same reasoning does not apply.
- The learning-styles claim should be treated as unsupported for modality matching; "preference" may still matter for motivation but is not shown to matter for comprehension.

### Gaps
- Dyslexia and ADHD: I did not find a verified silent-reading-rate meta-analysis for adults with dyslexia, nor a pooled-effect meta-analysis of ADHD and reading comprehension. Search summaries mentioned a 2020 meta-analysis of 178 studies (Reis et al.) finding large deficits in adults with dyslexia on word, pseudoword, passage reading, comprehension and spelling, an Italian adult-dyslexia study reporting silent reading about 6.15 syllables/s vs about 10.75-11.62 in skilled readers, and a 2022 scoping review of 34 ADHD studies in J Attention Disorders reporting impaired comprehension, but I could not map these to verified URLs and they are not relied on. Treat as leads.
- No data retrieved on prior knowledge x channel interactions or on WM capacity moderating reading vs listening specifically.
- Modality effects for people with hearing loss or visual impairment (outside scope) not covered.
- Older-adult and L2 effects are covered only through Brysbaert 2019 and one playback study.

### Cross-cutting inferences for the report-writer (all sections; inferences, not findings)
- A single construct "least taxing" does not exist in the measurement literature; the safe formulation is conditional: with self-paced control, at or below about 260 wpm for the individual, on text matched to their language skill and knowledge, reading and listening are comparable in comprehension, and the main controllable levers are pace control, repeat access and avoiding speeds past the individual's compression knee (about 1.5x for typical speech).
- Strongest evidence anchors: Brysbaert 2019 (rates), Clinton-Lisell (channel, pacing), Rayner et al. 2016 and Schotter 2014 (speed-accuracy, regressions), Coupé 2019 (speech information rate), Alhanbali 2019 and Shields 2023 (disagreement of effort measures). Weakest or disputed anchors: Zheng & Meister 10 bits/s, Christiansen & Chater quantification, the 275 wpm onset, the 2x playback result, and the cross-script efficiency comparisons.
