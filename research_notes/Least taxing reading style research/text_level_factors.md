# Text-level factors (words and sentences) that make reading least taxing

*Method note: about 40 searches/fetches in one session (cutoff Nov 2026). For many items only abstracts, publisher records or search snippets were readable (several PDFs and PMC pages returned binary data or bot-checks), so I tag each finding with how it was verified. Effect sizes are given only where I actually saw them; absent numbers are listed under Gaps rather than filled in from memory. Evidence-strength tags: [META] meta-analysis or large multi-dataset analysis; [REPL] replicated across labs/datasets; [SINGLE] single study or single lab; [CONTESTED] disputed or mixed; [ABSTRACT-ONLY] I saw only abstract/snippet/press. Population is skilled adult L1 readers unless flagged (CHILD, L2, LOW-SKILL).*

## Does a research field on this question exist, and what are its strands?

### Takeaway
Yes, but it is several partly disconnected fields rather than one. Basic psycholinguistics (eye movements, surprisal, memory-based parsing) measures processing cost word by word; applied readability and plain-language research measures whole-text outcomes (comprehension, recall, ratings). A 2025 paper explicitly tries to bridge them, which shows the gap is recognised.

### Cited Findings
- Strand 1, eye-movement/word-recognition research: length, frequency and predictability effects on fixations, fitted in regression models on corpora of natural reading — [Kliegl et al. 2004](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kliegl-grabner-rolfs-engbert-lengthfrequencyandpredictabilityeffectsofwordsoneyemovementsinreading-2004); [Kuperman & Van Dyke 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3119501); [Brysbaert et al. frequency review](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/0963721417727521).
- Strand 2, expectation-based / surprisal and computational psycholinguistics (Hale, Levy lineage): [Smith & Levy 2013](https://www.mit.edu/~rplevy/papers/smith-levy-2013-cognition.pdf); [Shain et al. 2024 PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576/); [Brothers & Kuperberg 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7584137); [Demberg & Keller 2008](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/~vera/CognitionDembergKeller08.pdf).
- Strand 3, memory-based sentence processing (dependency locality, cue-based retrieval, structural forgetting): [Futrell, Mahowald & Gibson 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4547262); [Gibson & Thomas 1999](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/870864712); [Jäger, Engelmann & Vasishth 2017](https://rdrr.io/github/vasishth/MetaAnalysisJaegerEngelmannVasishth2017/f/README.md); [Staub 2010](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/eye-movements-and-processing-difficulty-in-object-relative-clauses/811791906622144513-7415).
- Strand 4, good-enough processing, garden paths, non-canonical word order: [Christianson et al. 2001](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf); Ferreira 2003 (see passive section below).
- Strand 5, discourse/text comprehension (coherence, genre, prior knowledge): [McNamara & Kintsch 1996](https://scilit.com/publications/74c26bef2b7c9f5a1f1ef4c44095b868); [Mar et al. 2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/).
- Strand 6, readability formulas and their critics: [Benjamin 2012 review](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/885951890) (reviews two decades of readability work); a 2023 paper citing it says more than 200 readability models have been created since the 1940s ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/8436713/files/Assessing%20Readability%20Formulas%20in%20the%20Wild.pdf), snippet only); [Begeny & Greene 2014](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/can-readability-formulas-be-used-successfully-gauge-difficulty-reading); [Crossley, Greenfield & McNamara 2008 (Coh-Metrix)](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/237); [OneStop eye-tracking evaluation, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1).
- Strand 7, plain-language / document-design evaluation (legal, health, science summaries): [Martinez, Mollica & Gibson 2022](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/77d5b85e-5a7d-4dae-989d-a353d3ac2750); [Martinez et al. 2023 PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064); [Masson & Waldron 1994](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_58297814); [Stoll et al. 2022 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35666746/).
- Strand 8, processing fluency and social judgement of writers: [Oppenheimer 2006](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1178).
- Cross-cutting benchmark: silent reading rate meta-analysis, 190 studies, ~238 wpm for English non-fiction, ~260 wpm fiction — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf).
- The classic formulas were built by regressing sentence length, syllables per word and similar features on comprehension scores, not on real-time reading measures — [OneStop paper, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1) (explains why the two literatures are only loosely connected).

### Inferences
- There is no single "least taxing style" research programme. A writer-facing synthesis has to stitch together word-level eye-tracking, sentence-level parsing theory and applied plain-language trials, and the strands differ in how directly they measure "effort".
- Strands 1-2 give the most quantitatively reliable results (large corpora, many readers); strands 5-7 give the most decision-relevant outcomes (comprehension, recall) but with smaller, more heterogeneous samples.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a source that maps the whole field or states how much the strands cite each other; the "disconnected" characterisation is my judgement from the above.
- Background-knowledge leads not verified in this session (no source retrieved; treat as unconfirmed): Rayner 1998 and 2009 reviews; Hale 2001 / Levy 2008 original surprisal papers; Gibson 1998/2000 dependency locality theory; Kintsch construction-integration model; Kimble and Charrow & Charrow plain-language/legal work.

## Word-level properties: frequency, length, familiarity, predictability (surprisal)

### Takeaway
Word frequency, length and contextual predictability are the three best-established word-level predictors of fixation time and skipping in skilled readers; frequency acts on a roughly logarithmic scale, and the surprisal effect is most often found to be logarithmic, but its exact shape is contested. Effects are larger for lower-skill and L2 readers.

### Cited Findings
- [REPL] In natural reading, word length and frequency are reliably related to fixation durations and fixation probabilities; for words embedded in a corpus, predictability affected only the probability of fixating, but for designed target words all three factors affected first-pass durations and probabilities, and low predictability strongly increased second-pass reading — [Kliegl, Grabner, Rolfs & Engbert 2004](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kliegl-grabner-rolfs-engbert-lengthfrequencyandpredictabilityeffectsofwordsoneyemovementsinreading-2004). The follow-up replicates and extends these effects (and adds effects of neighbouring words) with 222 readers x 144 sentences — [Kliegl, Nuthmann & Engbert 2006](https://publishup.uni-potsdam.de/frontdoor/index/index/docId/5521) (abstract-level).
- [REPL] Typical reading fixations last about 200-250 ms with considerable variability across readers — [Springer 2011 article](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13414-011-0219-2.pdf) (snippet).
- [SINGLE] Frequency effects on first-fixation duration appear as early as 145 ms after fixation onset in a survival analysis of normal reading — [Southampton record](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/348380) (abstract-level).
- [META/REVIEW] The word frequency effect follows a compressed, roughly logarithmic curve; more than half of the effect lies below the intuitive start of the scale (1 occurrence per million), so differences among rare words matter more than differences among common ones. The effect is stronger in L2 processing, but the L1-L2 difference nearly vanishes once vocabulary size is entered as a predictor. (An earlier 2011 review by the same group recommended frequency corpora of 20M+ words validated against processing data.) — [Brysbaert et al. updated review](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/0963721417727521) (snippet; that aggregator page was flagged as sometimes mixing in unrelated abstracts, so verify against the journal version, Curr. Dir. Psychol. Sci.). A word prevalence resource (share of people who know each word) exists for 62,000 English lemmas — [Brysbaert et al. 2019](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/word-prevalence-norms-for-62000-english-lemmas/) (resource description only).
- [SINGLE, LOW-SKILL, ages 16-24, not college-bound, N=71] Reader skill (rapid naming, word identification) was the only reliable participant predictor across the time course and strongly modulated length and frequency effects. Between lowest and highest word-identification scorers, the gaze-duration cost of an 8- vs 4-character word was about 90 ms vs about 40 ms, and total fixation time about 160 vs about 90 ms; gaze durations rose for words longer than 6 letters in poor readers but only beyond 9 letters in skilled readers; highest vs lowest word-identification scorers differed by about 124 ms in gaze duration (main effect). The tool summary also listed "baseline" length +190 ms and frequency -58 ms effects on gaze duration, but the scale/units of those two figures were not clear — [Kuperman & Van Dyke 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3119501).
- [CONTESTED] The same authors later argued that corpus-based frequency counts overestimate word-representation strength in the low-frequency range and for small vocabularies; with subjective frequency ratings the skill x frequency interaction disappeared and skilled readers showed a uniform speed advantage — Kuperman & Van Dyke 2013 JEP:HPP, seen only in a search summary ([McMaster record, link inferred from search listing](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/handle/11375/22966)).
- [REPL/META-scale] Predictability (surprisal): Smith & Levy 2013 report a logarithmic relation between predictability and reading time across six orders of magnitude of estimated predictability, using a language model and two reading datasets — [Smith & Levy 2013](https://www.mit.edu/~rplevy/papers/smith-levy-2013-cognition.pdf) (abstract-level; PDF unreadable). Shain et al. 2024 re-test with six public datasets (self-paced reading, Maze, eye tracking; >2.2 million data points; Dundee 10 readers, GECO 14, Provo 84, Natural Stories SPR 178 etc.) and five language models (n-gram, PCFG, GPT-2, GPT-J, GPT-3) and conclude the effect is logarithmic — [Shain et al. 2024 PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576/). The ms-per-bit slope was not in the readable text.
- [CONTESTED] Brothers & Kuperberg 2021 report a robust linear relation between cloze predictability and word processing time in two high-powered experiments (self-paced reading N=216; cross-modal picture naming N=36) plus a meta-analysis of prior eye-tracking studies (N=218) — [JML 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7584137). Counter-evidence: a GPT-2-based reanalysis of the same data finds the cloze effect linear on a raw-probability scale but the language-model effect superlinear, favouring logarithmic — [Shain et al., HSP 2024 abstract](https://hsp2024.github.io/abstracts/submission_250.pdf) (conference abstract). A 2026 JEP:LMC eye-tracking replication (Buggy et al.) reportedly found a logarithmic relation for first fixation duration — only seen through a [FoRRT replication-atlas listing](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.jml.2020.104174/), unverified.
- [SINGLE corpus] Surprisal vs structural cost: in Dundee eye-tracking data (10 readers, 51,000 words of newspaper text), an unlexicalised surprisal measure predicted reading times for arbitrary words, whereas dependency-locality integration cost predicted only nouns, and the two measures were uncorrelated — [Demberg & Keller 2008](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/~vera/CognitionDembergKeller08.pdf). A 2021 CMCL paper agrees on integration cost but diverges on surprisal and notes the 2008 surprisal came from older PCFG estimates — [CMCL 2021](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2021.cmcl-1.21.pdf).
- [SINGLE dataset] In the OneStop eye-tracking data (360 L1 adult readers), mean per-word surprisal was the top measure for skip rate and regression rate and matched word length on reading speed and fixation duration; the effect of language-model size on surprisal's predictive correlations was small (beta about 0.002 per measure) — [arXiv 2502.11150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1).
- [META] Silent reading rate in English adults: about 238 wpm non-fiction, 260 wpm fiction (190 studies); the fiction advantage is attributed to shorter words; rates are lower for children, older adults and L2 readers — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf) (abstract-level, via search summary).

### Inferences
- For writers: choosing a common word over a rare synonym saves the most when the alternative sits in the very-low-frequency range; swapping between two already-common words matters little (follows from the log-scale frequency finding).
- Because predictability effects are at least monotone (linear or log) and context-dependent, the same word costs less in a context that makes it expected; word choice cannot be evaluated out of context.
- The fiction vs non-fiction speed gap (~22 wpm, ~9%: 260/238) is a rough real-world ceiling for what word-level easiness alone buys at the text level, assuming Brysbaert's word-length attribution holds.

### Gaps
- Canonical millisecond sizes of the high- vs low-frequency effect on gaze duration (Rayner/Inhoff/Duffy) could not be retrieved; one source used a 30 ms gaze-duration effect only as a worked illustration, which I did not treat as a finding.
- Familiarity / age-of-acquisition effects on reading: no usable source retrieved (only the Kuperman subjective-frequency point above).
- ms-per-bit surprisal slopes not retrieved (PDF/supplement unreadable).
- Effort measures beyond fixation time (pupil dilation, ERPs such as N400, self-reported effort): not searched.
- Dyslexia: no word-level source retrieved specifically on dyslexia (only the low-skill study above and a 2021 dyslexia paper on relative clauses found in a search listing, [PMC8307189](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8307189/), content not read).

## Sentence-level properties: length, syntactic complexity, garden paths, centre embedding, passive, negation, nominalisation

### Takeaway
Dependency-locality and centre-embedding costs are well motivated and supported by corpus and recall data, but direct reading-time support for the dependency locality theory in natural text is weaker than for surprisal. Garden-path misparses and non-canonical word order carry real, sometimes lingering costs; negation costs more unless context licenses it; evidence on sentence length and nominalisations as isolated factors is thin.

### Cited Findings
- [META-scale corpus] Across hand-parsed corpora of 37 languages in 10 families, observed dependency lengths are shorter than conservative random baselines, i.e. languages arrange words to keep heads and dependents close, which is consistent with a general processing pressure — [Futrell, Mahowald & Gibson 2015 PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4547262). Press coverage notes weaker minimisation in head-final languages (Japanese, Korean, Turkish) — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/linguistics-grouping-words-psychology-2369/); a non-peer-reviewed blog questions the family balance of the sample (24 of 37 Indo-European, unverified) — [Languagehat](https://languagehat.com/evidence-of-dependency-length-minimization/). This is evidence about language structure, not a direct reading-time measurement.
- [CONTESTED] Dependency locality theory (integration cost) did not reliably predict reading times for arbitrary words in the Dundee corpus (predicted only nouns, plus auxiliaries not predicted by the theory) — [Demberg & Keller 2008](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/~vera/CognitionDembergKeller08.pdf). In the OneStop data, integration cost, embedding depth and idea density were "very poor" predictors of the original-vs-simplified reading-ease difference — [arXiv 2502.11150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1) (caveat: original and simplified news paragraphs differ in many ways at once; simplified sentences were 16.9 vs 20.8 words on average, mean surprisal 4.77 vs 5.01).
- [REPL, qualitative] Object-extracted relative clauses are harder than subject-extracted ones; both violated expectations and memory-retrieval difficulty contribute, with qualitatively different behavioural signatures in eye movements — [Staub 2010](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/eye-movements-and-processing-difficulty-in-object-relative-clauses/811791906622144513-7415); framing as "widely accepted" in [PMC8307189](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8307189/).
- [META, heterogeneous] A Bayesian meta-analysis of 110 comparisons on similarity-based interference (eye tracking and self-paced reading) found facilitatory interference in subject-verb agreement and inhibitory interference in reflexives/reciprocals; the leading cue-based retrieval model fit only partly — [Jäger, Engelmann & Vasishth 2017](https://rdrr.io/github/vasishth/MetaAnalysisJaegerEngelmannVasishth2017/f/README.md) (abstract/README only; effect sizes not seen). Implication: having several similar nouns/clauses in play can hurt retrieval, but the effect depends on the construction.
- [CONTESTED] Centre embedding: the "missing VP" illusion shows doubly nested relative clauses are so costly that comprehenders rate versions with the middle verb phrase dropped no worse than grammatical ones, attributed to forgetting of the costliest prediction — [Gibson & Thomas 1999](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/870864712); replications are mixed: Dutch found no missing-VP effect and German shows a reversed pattern, possibly because verb-final order keeps verb predictions active (same source).
- [SINGLE x2, direct text test] In legal text, centre-embedded clauses reduced recall the most among the tested legalese features (rare jargon, centre embedding, passive voice, nonstandard capitalisation) in two experiments with 184 participants — [Martinez, Mollica & Gibson 2022 Cognition](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/77d5b85e-5a7d-4dae-989d-a353d3ac2750), as summarised in [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/poor-writing-not-legal-terms-is-what-makes-contracts-hard-to-understand) and [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-03-ways-lawyers-written-documents-easier.pdf). Corpus part: ~10 million words, contracts used these features far more than nine other genres.
- [REPL, qualitative] Garden paths: misinterpretation persists after reanalysis ("While Anna dressed the baby spit up on the bed": the baby is still partly interpreted as being dressed); this lingering is graded, not all-or-nothing — [Christianson et al. 2001](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf); supported by Patson et al. 2009 and Slattery et al. 2013 (same search listing, abstracts only).
- [CONTESTED] Passive and object-cleft sentences took longer and were misinterpreted more often than actives in thematic-role decision tasks (good-enough processing) — Ferreira 2003 Cognitive Psychology, as summarised in [Nottingham repository paper](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/5565210/OnlineRepresentationsOfNonCanonicalSentencesACCEPTED.pdf) and [CUNY 2019 abstract](https://www.colorado.edu/event/cuny2019/sites/default/files/attached-files/c16_burnsky_staub.pdf). Disputed: Bader & Meng 2018 argue the probe task itself (cue-based retrieval demand) creates part of the error; broader probes (yes/no questions, plausibility ratings) show little to no misreading; Paolazzi and colleagues find only moderate misinterpretation effects for passives (same summaries). Passive voice was also one of the bundled features that lowered recall/comprehension in the legalese experiments (cannot be isolated there) — [Martinez et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064).
- [REPL, qualitative] Negation: negated sentences typically show longer reading and response times, lower accuracy and distinct ERP responses; the usual truth-value advantage is reduced or absent for negatives; context can remove much of the cost (negation is integrated without delay when pragmatically licensed by prior context) — [Nieuwland & Kuperberg 2008](https://kuperberg.mgh.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/nieuwlandkuperberg_psychsci_08.pdf); review framing in [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02184/pdf) (search summary). Two theoretical accounts compete (delayed two-step simulation vs pragmatic account); context-free incrementality remains open.
- [SINGLE x2, thin] Nominalisation: in a corpus-based eye-tracking study of German legal text, nominalised words had longer reading times than other nouns and passages rewritten to use verbal structures were read faster overall — [Wolfer 2016](https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2016/docId/5647) (abstract-level). In an Italian study with 40 readers (workshop abstract) comprehension accuracy was at ceiling with no differences, but nominalised passages produced longer fixations and more regressions — [Venagli et al. 2025](https://tmalsburg.github.io/ComprehensionWorkshop/abstracts/VenagliEtAl2025). A 2021 Serbian/English study concluded syntactic complexity is a major factor in processing deverbal nouns — [scindeks 2021](https://scindeks-clanci.ceon.rs/data/pdf/0048-5705/2021/0048-57052104381G.pdf) (snippet).

### Inferences
- Defensible, evidence-backed sentence rules: avoid centre-embedded clauses, avoid long gaps between related words (subject-verb separation), avoid temporarily ambiguous constructions that tempt a wrong first parse, and state negation with context when it is needed. The weakest-evidenced popular advice is "write short sentences" (see Gaps) and "avoid passives" (effect is contested and small-to-moderate).
- Nominalisation costs show up in processing measures (fixations, regressions) before they show up in comprehension accuracy, which may be at ceiling for skilled readers on short passages.
- The inconsistency between DLT-style cost metrics failing in naturalistic corpora and centre embedding clearly hurting in controlled recall suggests structural cost bites mainly in rare, extreme constructions, not in everyday syntax (my interpretation).

### Gaps
- No controlled study of sentence length as an isolated factor (holding syntax, vocabulary and cohesion constant) was retrieved; the only length numbers are the incidental 16.9 vs 20.8 words in OneStop. Also not retrieved: how shortening sentences interacts with cohesion/connectives (relevant because simplification can remove explicit links).
- Effect sizes (ms) for passive, negation and garden-path costs not retrieved.
- Pupillometry / fMRI evidence on syntactic load (e.g. Just & Carpenter) not searched.
- Nominalisation evidence rests on abstract-level reports in German/Italian/Serbian/Spanish; no English reading-time study or meta-analysis found.

## Readability formulas (Flesch, Flesch-Kincaid, Dale-Chall, Lexile, Coh-Metrix): what they predict and their limits

### Takeaway
The formulas capture only two surface correlates (word difficulty and sentence length), were fitted to comprehension scores of specific readers, and predict real-time reading ease and grade-level fit only moderately to poorly. Coh-Metrix-style cognitive indices and, in a recent test, mean word surprisal predict reading ease better than classic formulas.

### Cited Findings
- [SINGLE, ABSTRACT-ONLY, CHILD] Eight formulas applied to six passages and checked against students' oral reading fluency: only one formula matched about 79% of the time, seven of eight were under 49% accurate, the worst 17%; one passage was rated from grade 1 to grade 5 depending on the formula; a few formulas worked only at particular grade levels and most were more accurate for higher-ability readers — [Begeny & Greene 2014](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/can-readability-formulas-be-used-successfully-gauge-difficulty-reading) (press/abstract summaries: [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140108102449.htm)).
- [SINGLE, adult L1, large] On OneStop (360 L1 adults, 30 Guardian articles each in "Advanced" and "Elementary" versions), tested formulas were Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Dale-Chall, Gunning Fog, Coleman-Liau, SMOG, ARI, plus CAREC, CARES, CML2RI and psycholinguistic measures. Authors' conclusion: existing formulas are moderate-to-poor predictors of reading ease (speed, fixation duration, skip rate, regression rate). Coleman-Liau was the best traditional formula and beat the three modern formulas; many formulas did not significantly correlate with regression rate; idea density, integration cost and embedding depth were very poor; entropy, word length, frequency and surprisal matched or beat Coleman-Liau, with surprisal best for skipping and regressions. Surprisal correlated r = -0.43 with CLEAR-corpus easiness scores. Per-formula correlation values appear only in a figure I could not read; limited to adult L1 English news — [arXiv 2502.11150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1).
- [SINGLE, L2] Length of text matters: for very short material none of the traditional formulas predicted any eye-movement measure (a newer formula predicted only fixation duration); for longer texts traditional formulas predicted fixations and newer cognitively based formulas also predicted skipping and saccade amplitude — [Nahatame, Language Learning (Tsukuba news release)](https://www.tsukuba.ac.jp/en/research-news/20210927140001.html) (population presumed L2 readers; not confirmed).
- [SINGLE, L2] Three Coh-Metrix variables tied to cognitive processes (selected as exploratory) predicted L2 reading difficulty (Bormuth passages, Japanese readers, mean cloze scores) more accurately than traditional formulas; authors flag exploratory status and unclear generalisability — [Crossley, Greenfield & McNamara 2008, TESOL Quarterly](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/237) (abstract-level).
- A Spanish review found that standard formulas (word length, lexical density, subordination) are not always reliable indicators of comprehension difficulty, notably for nominal style — [Salamanca record](https://produccioncientifica.usal.es/documentos/691f68f83da2765d9c7cd073?lang=en) (snippet).
- Formula-guided rewriting helps but is not sufficient: three staged simplifications of contracts (simpler vocabulary and syntax) reliably raised paraphrase and question-answering scores, but overall comprehension stayed very low — [Masson & Waldron 1994](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_58297814).

### Inferences
- Formulas are acceptable coarse screens for gross difficulty differences across large text samples; they are unreliable as a target for rewriting or for ranking texts of similar difficulty (agrees with Begeny & Greene's grade-level inconsistency and the OneStop result).
- Because word length, word frequency and surprisal outperform composite formulas, the useful content of most formulas probably comes from their word-level components; sentence-length and syllable terms add little beyond that in the one adult L1 test (inference from a single dataset).

### Gaps
- No source retrieved on Lexile specifically (its inputs or validity), Dale-Chall's word list validity, or how Flesch correlates with comprehension in the original norming data.
- No source retrieved on the argument that optimising for formula scores can degrade text (e.g. removing connectives to shorten sentences); known to me as a standard criticism but unverified here.
- Begeny & Greene seen only through press/abstract summaries; participants were school children, so adult applicability is uncertain.
- Coh-Metrix indices (cohesion, referential overlap) and their incremental validity against surprisal were not examined.

## Plain-language interventions (legal, medical, government): do they improve comprehension, speed, effort?

### Takeaway
Controlled evidence shows plain rewriting improves recall and comprehension of legal text, including for lawyers (Martinez et al.), and a randomised trial found a restructured plain-language Cochrane summary improved understanding from 18% to 53% correct; but systematic-review-level evidence is heterogeneous and thin, many interventions bundle wording with layout, and plain rewriting alone did not make complex legal content well understood in the older Masson & Waldron study.

### Cited Findings
- [SINGLE pair, preregistered follow-up] Legalese features (centre-embedded clauses, low-frequency words, nonstandard capitalisation, passive voice) lowered recall and comprehension even for experienced readers; the authors read this as support for working-memory strain from poor writing rather than specialist concepts (the "specialised concepts" account is undermined) — [Martinez, Mollica & Gibson 2022](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/77d5b85e-5a7d-4dae-989d-a353d3ac2750) via [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/poor-writing-not-legal-terms-is-what-makes-contracts-hard-to-understand). Won the 2022 Ig Nobel Prize for literature — [Legal Technology](https://legaltechnology.com/scientific-paper-on-why-contracts-are-difficult-to-understand-wins-ig-nobel-prize/). A drafting consultant criticises it as a bird's-eye view that adds little and questions the enforceability measure (non-peer-reviewed) — [Adams on Contract Drafting](https://www.adamsdrafting.com/a-comparative-corpus-analysis-tells-us-nothing-we-dont-already-know/).
- [SINGLE, preregistered] 2023 PNAS: 105 lawyers (compared with 108 laypeople from the earlier study) read 12 pairs of ~150-word contract excerpts in legalese vs simplified form with identical content. Both groups recalled (beta = 0.360, P = 0.003) and comprehended (beta = 0.354, P < 0.001) the simplified versions better; lawyers outperformed laypeople overall (recall beta = 0.353; comprehension beta = 0.808) with no significant lawyer x register interaction (recall P = 0.360; comprehension P = 0.638). Experiment 2 (about 102-105 lawyers) rated simplified contracts higher in overall quality (beta = 1.705), equally enforceable (P = 0.717), authors more hireable (beta = 1.835), higher willingness to use as written (beta = 1.432) and higher predicted client-signature likelihood (beta = 1.232). The paper's own hypothesis tests favoured "copy-and-paste / convenience and tradition" and did not support curse-of-knowledge or in-group signalling — [Martinez et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064) (summary from the fetch tool; the paper has an unreconciled 102 vs 105 sample count). Press coverage reports recall of about 45% (lawyers) vs 38% (laypeople) and frames "curse of knowledge" as Gibson's prior expectation, which conflicts with the paper's own hypothesis test — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1054728), [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2023/new-study-lawyers-legalese-0529) (not verified against the paper's figure).
- [SINGLE, older, small n] 24 clerical staff and 24 age-matched students read four short contracts with three levels of plain-English redrafting; simpler vocabulary and structure raised paraphrasing and question-answering, but overall comprehension stayed very low; non-experts also struggled with legal concepts that conflicted with prior beliefs — [Masson & Waldron 1994](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_58297814) (participant detail from a later paper's description in the same search).
- [RCT, health] Randomised trial of the Cochrane plain-language summary vs the old format: understanding of benefits, harms and evidence quality rose from about 18% to 53% correct (median quiz score 1 to 3; P < 0.001), independent of education level, and the new format was preferred — [Santesso et al. 2015 J Clin Epidemiol](https://www.statschat.org.nz/2014/08/02/when-in-doubt-randomise-4/) (secondhand, quoting the abstract; my inference: the new format restructured the summary as well as simplifying wording, so effects are not attributable to vocabulary/syntax alone).
- [SYSTEMATIC REVIEW] Plain language summaries: 7,714 records screened, 90 articles included; designs too varied to identify firm criteria for high-quality summaries; few studies measured effects on understanding or knowledge — [Stoll et al. 2022 PLOS ONE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35666746/). Two randomised experiments on summaries of psychological meta-analyses stressed explaining or replacing technical terms and found too many statistical details reduced user experience — [Kerwer et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.771399) (abstract-level).
- [SINGLE] Reading speed: legal passages rewritten from nominal to verbal structures were read faster overall in a German eye-tracking study — [Wolfer 2016](https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2016/docId/5647) (abstract-level). OneStop's "Elementary" versions were measured for reading speed, but the size of the speed gain was not in the text I could read — [arXiv 2502.11150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1).

### Inferences
- The best-supported claim is on recall and comprehension of legal prose: rewriting out centre embedding, rare words, passives and capitalisation helps, with similar benefit for experts and laypeople.
- Plain language shows reliable benefit on text that is mainly hard because of style; it does not eliminate difficulty rooted in unfamiliar concepts (Masson & Waldron).
- Effort per se (time, pupil, self-reported load) is rarely measured in the plain-language trials I found; most outcomes are recall/comprehension or ratings.

### Gaps
- No randomised or controlled trials found for government forms/letters (compliance, response rates), or for medical consent forms, within this search; Flory & Emanuel-type consent reviews and Charrow & Charrow jury-instruction studies are leads, unverified.
- Santesso 2015 sample size (143 recalled from memory; search could not confirm) and full design not verified.
- No effect sizes in standard units (d, g) retrieved for plain-language interventions; only regression betas, percent correct.
- Time-on-task/speed effect of plain rewriting in English not retrieved.

## Does simpler text help experts, and do readers judge simpler writing as more credible?

### Takeaway
Experts in the domain benefited as much as laypeople from simpler legal text and preferred it, and writers using needlessly complex words were judged less intelligent in Oppenheimer's studies; the one important qualification is that high-knowledge readers can learn more from low-cohesion text because the extra inference work pays off.

### Cited Findings
- [SINGLE, preregistered] Lawyers' recall and comprehension improved with simplified text, with no significant expert x register interaction; lawyers rated simplified contracts as higher quality and as equally enforceable — [Martinez et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064). Lawyers' predictions of difficulty for laypeople did not differ significantly from laypeople's own ratings (same source).
- [SINGLE paper, several experiments, no replication found] Oppenheimer (2006, Applied Cognitive Psychology 20:139-156): across the first three experiments more complex texts led to lower judged intelligence of the author regardless of original essay quality; the effect was mediated by processing fluency; hard-to-read fonts also lowered judged intelligence; when an irrelevant cause of low fluency was obvious readers discounted it and over-compensated in the opposite direction; in a dissertation-abstract study, replacing nine-letter-or-longer words with shorter synonyms raised authors' rated intelligence — [Oppenheimer 2006 record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1178); descriptions via [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2005-11-good-style-plain-simple.html) and [Improbable Research](https://improbable.com/2015/10/26/atlantic-profile-of-ig-nobel-winner-oppenheimer-and-murky-academic-writing/). Effect sizes and sample sizes not retrieved. Note that font effects are outside this brief's scope; the text-level finding is the long-word one.
- [CONTESTED / qualified] Coherence-reversal: low-coherence text benefited high-knowledge readers on open-ended questions, while the high-coherence text gave an advantage on recall and multiple-choice for low-knowledge readers; the sorting-task result from Experiment 1 did not replicate in Experiment 2; reading times indicated the low-coherence text demanded more inference processing; one critique notes some of the experimental passages were nearly impossible to understand — [McNamara & Kintsch 1996, Discourse Processes](https://scilit.com/publications/74c26bef2b7c9f5a1f1ef4c44095b868) (abstract-level). O'Reilly & McNamara 2007 ("Reversing the reverse cohesion effect") exists on the topic but I read only its title ([listing](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/0163853X.2018.1460159)).
- Experts and word-level reading: skilled/high-skill readers show smaller length effects (see Word-level section; Kuperman & Van Dyke 2011), so word-level simplification buys proportionally less for fast readers, though it still helps — [PMC3119501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3119501).

### Inferences
- "Simple helps only low-literacy readers" is not supported for legal prose: the expert group gained as much. The cohesion-reversal work shows the opposite risk is also real: removing explicit links and elaborations ("dumbing down" content) can reduce learning for knowledgeable readers.
- Readers' preference and credibility judgements run in the same direction as processing ease (Oppenheimer; Martinez 2023 hireability ratings), so simpler writing is unlikely to cost credibility in these settings. The single-paper status of Oppenheimer's work means the size of the credibility effect is uncertain.

### Gaps
- No independent replication of Oppenheimer 2006 found in the results (a search specifically for replications returned only the original and press coverage); I could not confirm whether any replication or failed replication exists.
- Not retrieved: studies on easy science writing producing overconfidence ("seduction of easiness", Scharrer et al.), jargon and perceived credibility (Shulman et al.), expertise-reversal effect in instructional design (Kalyuga). These are leads only.
- No source retrieved on whether plain wording changes persuasion or trust in government/medical sources.

## Concrete vs abstract words, and narrative vs expository text

### Takeaway
Narrative text is understood and recalled better than expository text (meta-analysis, robust across moderators), and narrative is also read slightly faster per word, partly because of shorter words; concrete words give a small, reliable early-fixation advantage in sentence reading, but the evidence is mixed and there is no meta-analysis for reading in context.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Narrative vs expository: over 75 unique samples and over 33,000 participants; stories were more easily understood and better recalled than essays; robust to leave-one-out checks and not moderated by study characteristics. Proposed reasons: expository organisation varies by purpose so readers lack a template, and expository text often carries unfamiliar concepts and vocabulary (so genre is confounded with familiarity) — [Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta 2021, Psychon Bull Rev](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/) (snippet/abstract; the PMC full text was inaccessible, so no effect-size figure).
- [META] Reading rate: non-fiction about 238 wpm vs fiction about 260 wpm; word length accounts for the difference — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf).
- [SINGLE, MIXED] Concreteness in natural sentence reading had a small but reliable effect on early fixation durations (first-fixation), additive with predictability (no interaction) — [Magnabosco & Hauk 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10962710/). A regression study found the concreteness effect on first fixation, gaze and total duration depended on which frequency measure was in the model (Juhasz & Rayner, as cited there). The concreteness advantage in naming and lexical decision seems to disappear in predictable sentence contexts (Schwanenflugel and colleagues, as cited there). A replication reports faster processing for concrete and emotional words on all fixation measures — [Yao et al., Cognition and Emotion](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/222234).

### Inferences
- Narrative advantage is robust enough to treat as established at the level of the whole text, but because genre bundles story structure, familiarity, vocabulary and word length, it does not imply that adding narrative to expository text will help; it implies that story-like, familiar structure and everyday vocabulary are easier.
- Concreteness is probably a minor lever for skilled adults in context compared with frequency, length and syntax; its benefit seems largest for isolated words and unpredictable contexts (inference from the Schwanenflugel point).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes (Hedges' g) for Mar et al. 2021 not retrieved (PMC page returned a bot check).
- No meta-analysis of concreteness effects in reading connected text; no source retrieved on concreteness effects on text-level comprehension, interest or recall (the Sadoski line of research).
- Whether the narrative advantage holds for expert readers or in L2/low-literacy readers: not retrieved.

## Overall ranking: which text properties matter most, and how strong is the evidence?

### Takeaway
Word frequency/length and syntax that forces long-distance memory (centre embedding, garden paths) are the best-evidenced determinants of reading cost; formulas, sentence length per se, passives and nominalisations are the weakest-evidenced popular levers.

### Cited Findings
- (Summary of the sections above; every item below is cited in the section named.)
- Strongest, replicated, quantitative: frequency, length, predictability effects on fixations (Word-level section; [Kliegl 2004](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kliegl-grabner-rolfs-engbert-lengthfrequencyandpredictabilityeffectsofwordsoneyemovementsinreading-2004), [Shain 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576/)); narrative vs expository ([Mar 2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/)).
- Strong but with a disputed functional form: predictability/surprisal (log vs linear) ([Brothers & Kuperberg 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7584137) vs [Shain 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10927576/)).
- Supported by few direct tests: centre embedding and bundled legalese features ([Martinez 2022](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/77d5b85e-5a7d-4dae-989d-a353d3ac2750), [2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064)); garden-path lingering ([Christianson 2001](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf)); negation costs moderated by context ([Nieuwland & Kuperberg 2008](https://kuperberg.mgh.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/nieuwlandkuperberg_psychsci_08.pdf)).
- Contested or thin: passive voice ([Ferreira 2003 and critiques](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/5565210/OnlineRepresentationsOfNonCanonicalSentencesACCEPTED.pdf)); nominalisation ([Wolfer 2016](https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2016/docId/5647), [Venagli 2025](https://tmalsburg.github.io/ComprehensionWorkshop/abstracts/VenagliEtAl2025)); dependency locality in natural reading ([Demberg & Keller 2008](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/~vera/CognitionDembergKeller08.pdf)); readability formulas ([Begeny & Greene 2014](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/can-readability-formulas-be-used-successfully-gauge-difficulty-reading), [arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1)); Oppenheimer credibility effect (single paper).

### Inferences
- Practical priority for a style recommendation, ordered by combined strength and likely effect: (1) common, short words; (2) no centre embedding and short dependencies; (3) avoid misleading first parses; (4) keep text predictable and story-like where possible; (5) lesser, contested items: passive, nominalisation, sentence length.
- Evidence on reader effort (as opposed to time and recall) is sparse in what I retrieved.

### Gaps
- Population coverage: most findings are English-speaking adults; dyslexia, low literacy and L2 findings are only touched (L2 frequency effect in Brysbaert; Kuperman & Van Dyke for low-skill young adults).
- This session did not retrieve any 2026 publication beyond the Buggy et al. replication pointer; the research-to-November-2026 window is under-covered.
