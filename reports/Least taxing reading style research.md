# Cutting Words Rarely Cuts Reading Effort

No single style is least taxing for every reader, and nobody has directly tested the telegraphic, article-stripped prose you are most curious about, but the neighbouring evidence points one way: reading effort tracks how many rare words, structural dependencies, ambiguities and unresolved inferences a text forces on the reader, not how many words it contains. Empirical research on the wider question does exist, in at least eight loosely connected fields (eye-movement psycholinguistics, surprisal theory, sentence-parsing theory, discourse comprehension, readability formulas, plain-language trials, multimedia learning, and speech-rate research), but they measure different things and rarely cite each other. For terse text, I found **no controlled experiment on function-word-stripped prose measuring reading time, comprehension and effort in skilled adult readers**; the closest evidence implies a modest speed gain, little saving per word because short function words are already skipped, and a real inference cost when stripped connectives leave structure ambiguous. Terseness pays off mainly for expert readers on predictable content and costs most for novices and for difficult material. The best-supported ways to lower reader effort are common short words, predictable content, no centre-embedded or garden-path sentences, explicit causal and contrastive connectives for non-experts, headings and signals (g of about 0.4 on learning), and delivery the reader can pace and re-read. Channel matters little: reading versus listening differs by g = 0.07 across 46 studies. "Least taxing" is not one quantity, because speed, accuracy and felt effort are measured by instruments that barely agree with each other. Much of this evidence was available to the research team only at abstract level, so the effect sizes below are indicative rather than final.

## Nobody has tested caveman prose, and the neighbouring evidence predicts small gains

The direct test does not exist. Searches for experiments that systematically delete function words from ordinary prose and then measure reading time, comprehension and effort in skilled readers found none. The nearest is an unpublished University of Maryland thesis on Braille readers, where removing 20% of words **raised reading rate**, scaffolding raised memory for story details, and, tellingly, user preference matched neither speed nor recall ([Eskenazi 2011](https://doi.org/10.13016/m2g424)). Tactile reading has a different bottleneck from print, and the study was not peer reviewed, so it shows direction at best.

Three lines of evidence explain why the savings should be modest. First, short function words are cheap already: skilled readers skip three-letter words about **67% of the time versus about 20% for seven- and eight-letter words** ([Rayner et al. 2011](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/181115/1/Rayner%252C_Slattery%252C_Drieghe%252C_%2526_Liversedge_%25282011%2529.pdf)), and a secondary account of Rayner's review puts fixation rates at about 85% for content words versus about 35% for function words ([secondary source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/12432282/)). Deleting words the eye mostly skips removes little processing. Second, reading time scales with the number of propositions in a sentence rather than the number of words (Kintsch and Keenan 1973, seen only through a [secondary summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/reading-comprehension-in-polish-and-english/higherlevel-language-processing-discourse-level-and-text-structure-knowledge/93977154CB030B045F4886E7551A6809)), so stripping grammatical glue lowers word count without lowering the semantic work. Third, reading time rises **logarithmically with surprisal**, confirmed across six datasets and over 2.2 million data points ([Shain et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2307876121)); highly predictable words cost almost nothing, so cutting them saves almost nothing, while any rise in surprisal at the next word, now less structurally certain, claws some of it back.

The costs come from the minority of cases where the dropped word was doing parsing work. Skilled readers silently repair isolated omissions: a repeated "the" went unnoticed on more than half of trials, and the median reader of full newspaper articles caught **only 1 of 9 inserted function-word errors** ([Staub et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-018-1492-z); [Staub et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02586-1)). That shows these words are usually redundant given context, but it was tested on rare random errors in normal text, not on a whole register of omissions. Where context does not rescue the reader, compression bites. Comprehenders weigh plausibility against a noise model and will adopt a non-literal reading when the literal one looks implausible ([Gibson, Bergen and Piantadosi 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216438110)); the standard test case, newspaper headlinese, produced ambiguities that readers often failed to register ([Perfetti et al. 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596x(87)90110-0), abstract not verified). Letter-level compression is costly even for experts: skilled text-message users read abbreviated sentences more slowly than normal ones ([Perea, Acha and Carreiras 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210902783653)).

Recent LLM-era work adds only weak signals. In a 4,563-person randomised study, LLM-simplified text raised multiple-choice accuracy by **3.9 percentage points** and perceived ease by 0.33 on a five-point scale, but that was simplification, not telegraphic compression ([Guidroz et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01980)). The one human test of aggressively compressed text, a machine-oriented format condensed to 27.9% of original length, reported lower human question-answering accuracy with the magnitude shown only in a figure ([Zhu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19857)). In a 24-professional study, very short LLM reasoning outputs were the hardest to check for errors (an incorrect answer was correctly rejected only 24.5% of the time, against 54.2% for medium length), though the confidence intervals overlap ([Mountford et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06878v1)). All three are preprints.

The honest reading, by outcome: raw reading time per text falls somewhat; comprehension of predictable, low-ambiguity content holds; comprehension of structurally ambiguous or inference-heavy content drops; and felt effort and preference can diverge from performance in either direction. One steelman deserves weight. Readers adapt to the noise rate they encounter ([Gibson et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1216438110)), so a reader fluent in a terse register, such as a developer reading commit messages, may pay little. No study has tested that adaptation for telegraphic registers.

## Common words and tidy syntax carry the strongest evidence

Where the literature is solid, it concerns words. Word length, frequency and contextual predictability independently shape fixation time and skipping in natural reading ([Kliegl et al. 2004](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kliegl-grabner-rolfs-engbert-lengthfrequencyandpredictabilityeffectsofwordsoneyemovementsinreading-2004)). Frequency works on a compressed, roughly logarithmic scale in which more than half the effect lies below one occurrence per million, so swapping a rare word for a common one helps far more than swapping between two common ones ([Brysbaert et al. review](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/0963721417727521)). Skill moderates it: among low-skill young adults, an eight-letter word cost about 90 ms more gaze time than a four-letter one for the weakest readers versus about 40 ms for the strongest ([Kuperman and Van Dyke 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3119501)). The ceiling for word-level ease alone is visible in natural text: English fiction is read at about 260 words per minute against 238 for non-fiction, a gap of roughly 9% that word length explains, in a meta-analysis of 190 studies and 18,573 participants ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)). Whether the surprisal effect is logarithmic or linear is still argued ([Brothers and Kuperberg 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7584137)), but all sides agree it is monotone: the more expected the word, the cheaper.

At sentence level the evidence thins quickly. The best-supported cost is memory strain from centre embedding: in two experiments with 184 participants, centre-embedded clauses reduced recall more than rare jargon, passive voice or odd capitalisation in legal prose ([Martinez, Mollica and Gibson 2022](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/77d5b85e-5a7d-4dae-989d-a353d3ac2750)). Garden-path misparses leave lingering misinterpretations even after the reader reanalyses ([Christianson et al. 2001](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf)), and negation slows reading unless the context licenses it ([Nieuwland and Kuperberg 2008](https://kuperberg.mgh.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/nieuwlandkuperberg_psychsci_08.pdf)). The popular rules are the weakly evidenced ones. Passive costs are contested, because part of the measured error comes from the probe task ([Nottingham repository summary of Ferreira 2003 and critiques](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/5565210/OnlineRepresentationsOfNonCanonicalSentencesACCEPTED.pdf)). Nominalisation raises fixations and regressions but not accuracy in small Italian and German studies ([Venagli et al. 2025](https://tmalsburg.github.io/ComprehensionWorkshop/abstracts/VenagliEtAl2025); [Wolfer 2016](https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/year/2016/docId/5647)). **No controlled study isolates sentence length** while holding vocabulary, syntax and cohesion constant, so "write short sentences" rests on correlation and craft lore.

That also explains why readability formulas disappoint. In the OneStop eye-tracking data (360 adult readers), Flesch, Fog, SMOG and the rest were moderate-to-poor predictors of reading ease, with the simple Coleman-Liau best among them, and mean word surprisal matched or beat all of them ([OneStop, arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11150v1)). With children, seven of eight formulas matched oral-reading difficulty less than 49% of the time ([Begeny and Greene 2014](https://readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/can-readability-formulas-be-used-successfully-gauge-difficulty-reading)). Formulas are coarse screens, not rewriting targets, and optimising sentence length can strip the connectives the next section shows readers need.

Plain-language rewriting works, including for experts. In a preregistered study, 105 lawyers recalled (beta = 0.360) and comprehended (beta = 0.354) simplified contract excerpts better than legalese, with no expert-by-register interaction ([Martinez et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266064)). A randomised trial of a restructured Cochrane summary lifted correct understanding from about 18% to 53%, though the redesign changed layout as well as wording ([Santesso et al. 2015, via secondary summary](https://www.statschat.org.nz/2014/08/02/when-in-doubt-randomise-4/)). Simpler words and syntax raised contract comprehension in an older study too, but absolute scores stayed very low because non-experts lacked the underlying legal concepts ([Masson and Waldron 1994](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2350080107)). Style fixes style-caused difficulty and nothing else. Two further levers have support: stories are understood and recalled better than essays across **75-plus samples and 33,000-plus participants** ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/)), though genre bundles structure, familiarity and word length; and writers who use needlessly long words are judged less intelligent, in a single-paper finding without independent replication ([Oppenheimer 2006](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1178)).

## Connectives and elaboration help novices, while terseness rewards experts

The telegraphic question is really a question about cohesion, and that literature has a clear moderator: what the reader already knows. In biology texts, low-knowledge junior-high readers learned more from coherent text while high-knowledge readers learned more from a minimally coherent one, which the authors attributed to the extra inference work the sparse text forced ([McNamara et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1)). Reading times showed the low-coherence text demanded more inference processing, and one sorting-task result failed to replicate within the same paper ([McNamara and Kintsch 1996](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539609544975)). The "reverse cohesion effect" then narrowed further: in 143 college students it held only for less-skilled high-knowledge readers, while skilled high-knowledge readers did better with the cohesive text ([O'Reilly and McNamara 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530709336895)). Sparse text can deepen learning for the knowledgeable and skilled, but it does so by making them work harder, which is the opposite of reducing effort.

Not every connective earns its place. Across 794 Dutch adolescents and 20 authentic texts, adding causal and contrastive connectives raised comprehension while additive ones lowered it, and effects were local rather than global ([Kleijn, Pander Maat and Sanders 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2019.1605257)). Repairing causal gaps helped both skill levels, but only for the difficult text ([Linderholm et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1804_4)). In second-language reading, elaboration (added redundancy and explicit relations) beat simplification in 180 Korean students ([Oh 2001](https://doi.org/10.2307/3587860)). A meta-analysis of 60 studies and 5,924 participants gives the general rule: high-assistance instruction beat low-assistance for low-knowledge learners (d = 0.505), and the reverse held for high-knowledge learners (d = -0.428), with novices harmed more than experts gained ([Tetzlaff et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142)). "Assistance" there spans worked examples and explanation broadly, not wording alone.

Concision does win when it removes irrelevant material rather than grammatical glue. Interesting-but-off-topic "seductive details" reduce learning by about g = -0.33 across 58 studies ([Sundararajan and Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4)), and summaries of main points beat full textbook chapters for retention at 20 minutes, one week and six to twelve months ([Reder and Anderson 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(80)90122-x)). These are different operations from telegraphic compression and should not be conflated with it: cutting a digression lightens the load, cutting "because" adds to it.

Controlled technical English and clinical shorthand bracket the practical extremes. Boeing maintenance procedures rewritten in Simplified English were comprehended significantly better by 130 engineering students, but only for the complex procedure, with no reading-time difference ([Shubert et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.2190/wg69-d74b-4dll-2wbk)). Simplified English restricts vocabulary and structure to cut ambiguity; it is not telegraphic, so it neither supports nor refutes stripping function words. Clinical abbreviations show the decoding cost of idiosyncratic compression: in one survey of 46 clinicians, the share giving the intended meaning ranged from **0 to 87% depending on the abbreviation** ([Jayatilake and Oyibo 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10479966)). The pattern across all of these is that benefit grows with text difficulty and reader knowledge gaps, which is the same moderator that appears in the expertise-reversal data.

## Layout aids recall and search, but the folklore outruns the data

Format evidence is real but thinner than design advice implies. There is no meta-analysis of bullets versus prose. Five studies of everyday functional texts found bulleted lists improved recall of listed items in three studies and reader evaluation in two, but the benefit weakened when listed items were heterogeneous and in two studies bullets worsened recall of the surrounding text ([Jansen 2014](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan), abstract only). Lists also beat paragraphs for medication instructions in both young and older adults ([Morrow et al. 1998](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/4ee813b1-7046-4e08-83c5-5b6d9861be60)). The consistent reading is that lists suit parallel, homogeneous items and strip the "because" and "unless" relations that heterogeneous ideas need, though that mechanism is inferred rather than directly tested.

Signalling has the best quantitative support. An overview of 29 reviews covering 1,189 studies put signalling's effect on learning at **g = 0.43 and on cognitive load at g = 0.25**, though another review found a load effect of only 0.11 and most of the evidence involves multimedia cues rather than headings in plain text ([Noetel et al. 2022](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8zqy4/multimedia-design-for-learning-an-overview-of-reviews-with-meta-meta-analysis)). For headings in prose, the Lorch programme finds that free recall and summarisation usually improve, topic-search is much faster with informative headings, and recognition tests usually show no effect ([Lemarié, Lorch and Péry-Woodley 2012](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/8600)). Tables beat prose for comparison: a table-based fact box improved comprehension in a preregistered sample of 2,305 (d reported as 0.39 in a secondary summary) ([Brick, McDowell and Freeman 2020](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.190876)). Conclusion-first ordering has no direct support: experiments on news find the inverted pyramid no better, and sometimes worse, for recall than chronological order, and no controlled test of "bottom line up front" in business writing was found ([Journalism Studies 2017 record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/1461670X.2017.1324316)). Its best justification is that scanners can stop early. Paragraph length has essentially no controlled evidence.

Typography matters less than designers assume. A review of 42 empirical studies found serif versus sans-serif made no reliable difference and no type size or typeface is best for everyone ([Ho Sang and Petrarca 2025](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/download/8855/7099/13208)). Lines of about 55 characters gave the best comprehension in one classic study, while longer lines read faster ([Dyson and Haselgrove 2001, via summary](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html)). The best and worst font for an individual differed by about 35% in reading speed, and stated preference did not predict which was fastest ([Wallace et al. 2022](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)). Deliberately hard-to-read fonts do not improve learning: a meta-analysis of 25 articles and 3,135 participants found d = -0.01 on recall ([Xie, Zhou and Liu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x)). Screens cost a little relative to paper (g = -0.21 across 54 comparisons and 171,055 participants, concentrated in time-limited reading of informational text) ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003)), with reading time itself not reliably different ([Clinton 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269)).

Treat web-writing statistics with suspicion. The widely quoted "124% usability improvement" for concise, scannable, objective copy comes from 81 participants, one site, one-tailed tests and a composite sensitive to a tiny control-group error rate, and its authors wrote that more research was needed ([Morkes and Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)). The F-pattern has no published statistics ([Nielsen 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)), and a vendor study of 99 websites found no individual heat map resembling an F ([EyeQuant 2017](https://eyequant.com/resources/eye-tracking-studies-does-the-famous-f-shape-pattern-really-exist)). What replicates is that web readers sample rather than read, with time on a page growing only weakly with its length ([Weinreich et al. 2008, via NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)).

## Channel hardly matters, pace control does, and effort has no single gauge

Reading versus listening is close to a draw for skilled adults. A meta-analysis of 46 studies and 4,687 participants found **g = 0.07 (no reliable difference)**, with self-paced reading modestly ahead (g = 0.13) and reading ahead on inferential questions (g = 0.36) ([Clinton-Lisell, Review of Educational Research](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1476650676912693248)). Adding audio to text helps only when reading is experimenter-paced (g = 0.41) and not when it is self-paced (g = 0.06) ([Clinton-Lisell 2023](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1403866.pdf)). In 91 adults, audiobook, e-text and both gave no comprehension differences, immediately or after two weeks ([Rogowsky, Calhoun and Tallal 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/sagope/v6y2016i3p2158244016669550.html)). The consistent moderator is who controls the pace and whether the reader can revisit, because listening is by default system-paced and non-revisitable (this is an interpretation of the pattern). Brain data agree on the semantics: tuning maps for the same stories heard or read are interchangeable ([Deniz et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6764208)). Matching modality to a reader's "learning style" has no support ([Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)), though for struggling readers text-to-speech helps (d = .35) ([Wood et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021/)).

Speed has a ceiling either way. Skilled adults read silently at **238 to 260 words per minute** with a normal range of 175 to 300, and Brysbaert notes this sits right at the roughly 260 wpm limit at which compressed speech stays understood, suggesting a shared late bottleneck rather than anything reading-specific ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)). Speed-reading claims fail the same test: comprehension falls as speed rises, and forbidding re-reading drove question accuracy from about 75% to chance, about 50% ([Rayner et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1529100615623267); [Schotter, Tran and Rayner 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614531148)). Sped-up lectures cost little to 1.5x and moderately to heavily at 2x or more in a 24-study meta-analysis ([Tharumalingam et al. 2025](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/increasing-video-lecture-playback-speed-can-impair-test-performance-a-meta-analysis/1129892227363897361-10463)), although an earlier experiment with 231 students found little harm at 2x ([Murphy et al. 2022](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)). Across 17 languages, spoken information rate converges near 39 bits per second because fast languages carry less per syllable, so no language is established as more efficient ([Coupé et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6984970)). Mind wandering is the hidden tax: it correlates about -0.21 with comprehension across 25 texts ([Bonifacci et al. 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/35840838/)), and listening to a read passage produced the most of it in one within-subject study ([Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00892/pdf)).

Effort itself resists measurement, which is why no one can say a style is "least taxing" without naming a dimension. In 116 adults, pupil size, EEG alpha, skin conductance and self-rated effort were weakly or non-significantly correlated even though each was reliable, so the authors warn that the measures should not be used interchangeably ([Alhanbali et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7664710/)); a review of 48 papers found only **36.1% of 274 correlations significant** ([Shields et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9982391)). Most of that validation comes from hearing research, not reading. The core language network responds to sentence surprisal and well-formedness ([Tuckute et al. 2024](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/153265)), while the generic multiple-demand effort network engages mostly when a task is imposed, not during passive reading ([Diachek et al. 2020](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/133763)). Much measured "effort" is therefore the cost of the questions asked about the text.

### Ranking the levers by strength of evidence

| Lever | Direction | Evidence strength | Main caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common, shorter words | Faster, easier | Strong, replicated | Gains shrink for skilled readers |
| No centre embedding, short dependencies, no garden paths | Better recall, fewer misparses | Moderate, few direct tests | Costs concentrated in extreme constructions |
| Self-paced, re-readable delivery | Protects comprehension | Strong (pacing moderator, speed-accuracy) | Individual spread is about 60 wpm |
| Causal and contrastive connectives | Helps novices, hard texts | Moderate | Additive connectives can hurt |
| Headings and signals | Better recall, faster search | Moderate (g about 0.4) | Mostly multimedia; recognition tests show nothing |
| Lists and tables | Better recall and comparison | Weak to moderate | Strips relations; heterogeneous items |
| Telegraphic compression | Faster per text, more inference | Indirect only | No direct test; expert readers, predictable content |
| Reading versus listening | About equal | Strong null (g = 0.07) | Depends on who controls pace |
| Font, justification, paragraph length | Small or unknown | Weak | Preference does not predict performance |

## Conclusion

The tidy answer that terse writing is easier because it is shorter does not survive contact with the evidence. Reading time follows propositions and surprisal, not word count, and the words telegraphic style discards are the cheap, predictable ones. What remains hard in compressed text is relational structure, which is why cutting a digression helps while cutting a connective hurts. A workable rule is to compress redundancy but keep structure, and to match that compression to the reader: experts reading predictable material tolerate or even benefit from it, novices facing difficult text do not. This rule is an inference from adjacent studies, not a tested result.

The more useful finding may be a negative one about the field. Performance and preference dissociate repeatedly (Braille readers, fonts), effort measures disagree, and the single direct experiment readers most want, a controlled comparison of telegraphic and full prose that measures reading time, comprehension and effort separately in novices and experts, has apparently not been run. Anyone who claims to know the answer for caveman-style text is extrapolating. Compressed styles are now being tried for prompts and LLM output, where token savings are measured for machine readers and human comprehension rarely is, so that missing experiment has practical urgency.
